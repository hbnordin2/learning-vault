# Cue-dependent recall

Successful retrieval from memory depends on the match between the cue presented at test and the information encoded at study. A flashcard's question is its retrieval cue. Whatever the question specifies is what memory is prompted to produce; material in the answer that the question does not cue is material the learner is not being tested on. This is the mechanism behind the most common card-quality failure: answers that carry more than the question asks for.

## Encoding specificity

Tulving and Thomson (1973) established the **encoding specificity principle**: a retrieval cue is effective only to the extent that it was encoded together with the target at study. A cue that was not part of the original encoding context cannot elicit the target, even if it is semantically related. The practical consequence is that recall is not a function of how much is "in" memory but of whether the right cue is present at test.

Morris, Bransford and Franks (1977) extended this with **transfer-appropriate processing**: memory performance depends on the match between the type of processing at encoding and the type demanded at retrieval. Studying for meaning helps on a meaning test; studying for sound helps on a rhyme test. For flashcards, this means the learner encodes the answer in terms of whatever the question asks for — and information in the answer that falls outside that frame is encoded weakly or not at all.

## The consequence for flashcard answers

When a card's answer contains more than the question cues, three things happen:

1. **The cued portion is tested; the trailing portion is re-read.** The learner retrieves the part the question asks for, then flips the card and *reads* the rest. Reading is not retrieval — it produces recognition, not recall, and builds little durable memory ([[Retrieval is reconstruction|retrieval modifies memory; re-reading does not]]).

2. **The learner misattributes failure.** The trailing material is visible on the answer side, so the learner perceives a gap between what they produced and what the card "expected." A card self-graded as failed may in fact reflect a correct answer to the question asked, plus the learner noticing they did not spontaneously produce bonus facts the question never prompted. This corrupts the SRS scheduling: pressing "Again" because of uncued material resets intervals on a card the learner actually knows.

3. **Encoding of the trailing material is shallow.** Because the trailing facts are encountered via re-reading after the retrieval attempt, they receive the weak encoding that [[The testing effect|the testing-effect literature]] shows is inferior to generation. Repeated exposure to them on the answer side creates a [[The metacognitive trap|fluency illusion]] — they look familiar, so the learner expects to be able to produce them — but familiarity is recognition, not recallable knowledge.

## The implication: the question fully determines what is learned

A card teaches what its question prompts. If a fact is worth learning, it needs a question that cues it directly. Trailing material that "hangs off the back" of an answer is not being spaced-repeated; it is being re-read on a schedule, which is the method the literature rates lowest ([[Learning|Dunlosky et al., 2013]]).

The fix is not to memorise more of the answer through effort. The fix is to write questions that fully specify their answers, and to extract uncued material into its own cards with its own questions — see [[Rewriting overloaded cards]].

## Evidence tier

- **Encoding specificity**: established by controlled experiment, replicated, and foundational to the memory literature. Not controversial.
- **Transfer-appropriate processing**: established by experiment (Morris et al., 1977), widely cited, occasionally debated in scope but not in its core claim.
- **Application to flashcard answer structure**: principled extrapolation from the above. No controlled study has directly compared "overloaded-answer cards" to "atomic cards" on long-term retention in an SRS context. The extrapolation is well-grounded but unvalidated as a specific flashcard-design finding.
