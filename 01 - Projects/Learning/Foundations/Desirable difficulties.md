# Desirable difficulties

The organising principle of the project. The term is Robert Bjork's (1994): a **desirable difficulty** is a condition that makes learning feel harder and depresses apparent progress *during* practice, while producing better long-term retention and transfer. The difficulties are desirable because of the storage/retrieval asymmetry in [[How memory works]] — effortful, lower-success retrieval builds more durable storage than smooth, easy processing. Almost every high-utility technique in this project is a specific desirable difficulty.

## The performance–learning distinction

The principle underneath the label is that performance during training is a poor, often negative, index of learning (Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015). Performance is how well the learner is doing right now; learning is the durable change that remains weeks later. They come apart constantly. Conditions that boost performance now (massing, rereading, keeping one topic in front of the learner) frequently *reduce* learning, and conditions that depress performance now (spacing, interleaving, testing) frequently *increase* it. Because the only thing available in the moment is performance, the sense of how learning is going is built on the wrong signal. This is the source of [[The metacognitive trap]].

## The catalogue

The established desirable difficulties, each with its own note:

- **Spacing** — distributing practice over time so retrieval strength has decayed before the next attempt. [[The spacing effect]].
- **Retrieval** — self-testing instead of restudying, so the answer is reconstructed rather than reread. [[The testing effect]].
- **Interleaving** — mixing different topics or problem types rather than blocking one at a time, which forces discrimination of which approach applies. [[Interleaving]].
- **Generation** — producing an answer (even a wrong one, with feedback) before being shown it, rather than passively reading. Covered under [[The testing effect]]; the generation effect (Slamecka & Graf, 1978) is its close relative, d ≈ 0.40.
- **Varying the conditions of practice** — changing context, examples, and surface form, which slows acquisition but improves transfer to new situations. See [[Transfer of learning]].
- **Reducing feedback frequency** — withholding immediate feedback in motor and procedural learning so the learner does more of the error-correcting.

## The conditionality — when a difficulty becomes undesirable

The popular renderings tend to drop this caveat. A difficulty is desirable only if the learner can surmount it. Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell (2013) state it plainly: if a learner lacks the prior knowledge to overcome the difficulty, or the material's [[Cognitive load and worked examples|intrinsic load]] already saturates working memory, the difficulty stops helping and starts blocking encoding. Retrieval practice with no feedback and a near-zero success rate teaches nothing. Interleaving categories a novice cannot yet tell apart produces confusion.

This is the same boundary as the [[Direct instruction vs discovery learning|novice-needs-guidance]] result: for a genuine novice in a domain, worked examples and explicit guidance beat struggle, and the difficulties become desirable only once enough schema is in place to make the struggle productive. The rule is not "make it harder" but "make it as hard as the learner can *just* handle" — difficulty calibrated to the edge of current ability.

## Status

The principle is robust as an organising frame, and the component effects it covers (testing, spacing, interleaving, generation) are each meta-analytically established. But "desirable difficulties" is a *label for a family of findings*, not a quantitative theory with a single effect size — and its accurate form always carries the surmountability caveat. Treat it as the lens that indicates which study conditions to trust, not as a licence to make everything maximally hard.
