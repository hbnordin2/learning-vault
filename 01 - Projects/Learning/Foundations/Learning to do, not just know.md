# Learning to do, not just know

There are two kinds of knowledge. **Knowing-that** is declarative: facts, frameworks, and vocabulary — what a good go-to-market strategy is, the steps of a process, the principles of good design. **Knowing-how** is procedural: producing a go-to-market strategy, evaluating it, and revising it into a good one. The two are stored in different brain systems and acquired differently, and procedural skill does not accumulate from declarative knowledge. Complete mastery of what is written about a domain can coexist with an inability to perform in it — a person can know the chess literature and still play badly. This is the foundational concept for the skill side of the project, paralleling [[How memory works]] on the fact side.

## The distinction is well established

The supporting evidence is a double dissociation — each capacity can be impaired while the other is spared, in both directions, which is the strongest available form of evidence.

- Ryle (1949) drew the philosophical distinction between knowing-how and knowing-that. The empirical support is neuropsychological.
- Patient H.M., after removal of his hippocampal region, could form no new declarative memories, yet improved across days of practice at mirror-drawing — a motor skill — while having no memory of the sessions (Milner, 1962).
- Cohen & Squire (1980) found the same pattern with mirror-reading; their paper is titled *Dissociation of knowing how and knowing that*. Amnesic patients acquired and retained the skill while failing to remember the material or the training.
- Knowlton, Mangels & Squire (1996) showed the double dissociation directly: on a probabilistic-classification task, amnesic patients learned the skill but could not recall the training, while Parkinson's patients showed the reverse. Declarative memory depends on the medial temporal lobe; procedural learning on the basal ganglia and cerebellum.

Anderson's ACT-R (1982) describes the link between the two. A skill usually begins as declarative knowledge — explicit rules followed slowly and effortfully under working-memory load — and becomes procedural through practice, a process called knowledge compilation, after which it runs automatically and is often hard to verbalise. Expert practitioners frequently cannot state the rules they follow, because those rules are no longer held declaratively. Declarative study installs the scaffold for a skill but cannot produce the skill itself; that requires performing the task.

## Production, evaluation, and iteration

Generative skills — designing a go-to-market strategy, a content strategy, a product or design strategy, or a piece of engineering — require producing an artifact in an ill-structured domain and improving it. Three capacities are involved:

- **Production** — generating a candidate.
- **Evaluation** — judging whether the candidate is good.
- **Iteration** — using that judgment to improve the next version.

This three-part division is an organising convenience rather than an established result, but each part connects to a grounded finding below.

## Evaluation is usually the constraint

Production is rarely the limiting factor, because generating another candidate is cheap. Improvement is limited by the ability to judge one's own output and by whether the environment supplies a corrective signal. Two findings make evaluation the constraint: self-assessment is systematically miscalibrated ([[The metacognitive trap]]), and low-validity environments withhold reliable feedback ([[Deliberate practice in fuzzy domains|Kahneman & Klein, 2009]]). This is an inference from those findings rather than a directly tested result.

A related asymmetry, held at moderate confidence: the ability to recognise quality often develops ahead of the ability to produce it. Developing the evaluative eye, by studying many strong examples, supports later production.

## Feedback validity varies by domain

Skill develops through experience only when the environment has stable relationships between cues and outcomes and supplies fast, clear feedback (Kahneman & Klein, 2009). The relevant domains differ sharply on this axis, which changes the appropriate method:

- **Engineering** — high validity, fast feedback. Code runs or fails, performance is measurable, and a design can be compared against known-good examples. The production–evaluation–iteration loop is tight, and skill develops through practice with the available feedback.
- **Product and design strategy** — moderate validity. Signals such as usage and retention exist but arrive slowly and are confounded.
- **Go-to-market and content strategy** — low-to-moderate validity. Feedback is slow, noisy, and multi-causal.
- **Market selection and large strategic bets** — low validity. Feedback arrives years later and is heavily confounded, and experience tends to build confidence without accuracy.

As validity falls, practice alone yields less skill, and feedback must be engineered — pre-registered predictions that are later scored, comparison against exemplars, and expert critique. This is covered in [[Deliberate practice in fuzzy domains]].

## Method, by stage

- **Install the declarative scaffold and study examples.** Make the relevant facts, vocabulary, and decision-rules automatic, through [[Spaced repetition systems|spaced retrieval]], so that working memory is free during production; this is a cognitive-load argument. Then study and deconstruct many strong examples, which builds both the schema and the evaluative eye. The [[Cognitive load and worked examples|worked-example effect]] supports this, with the caveat that it has been tested mainly on well-structured problems such as mathematics, so its application to open-ended strategy is an extrapolation.
- **Produce with scaffolding.** Begin from templates and worked examples rather than a blank page, and reduce the support as competence grows; this is guidance fading. Vary the cases produced — different products, markets, formats — to develop a general skill rather than a single template. The variability-of-practice evidence comes mainly from motor learning, so this is also an extrapolation to strategic work.
- **Evaluate against external standards.** Because self-assessment is unreliable, compare output against expert exemplars, where the difference between the two is the informative part (the [[Transfer of learning|contrasting-cases]] finding); against outcomes where they exist; and against expert critique.
- **Iterate by engineering feedback.** Where feedback is fast, as in engineering or advertising metrics, use it directly. Where it is slow or noisy, manufacture it: score pre-registered predictions, and run post-mortems that evaluate the reasoning rather than the outcome. Feedback is not uniformly beneficial — about a third of feedback interventions reduce performance (Kluger & DeNisi, 1996).

## Strength of the evidence

- The knowing-that and knowing-how distinction is well established, by double dissociation.
- That skill is built through practice with feedback, and benefits from early scaffolding such as worked examples and guidance fading, is grounded — but the supporting studies use well-structured problems, so the application to open-ended strategy is an extrapolation.
- The production–evaluation–iteration division, and the claim that evaluation is the usual constraint, are inferences from grounded findings rather than directly tested results.
- The science of training complex, ill-structured, generative skills is the least developed area in this field, far less developed than the science of fact retention. The claims in this note should be held more loosely than those on spacing and retrieval.
