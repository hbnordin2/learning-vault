# What the science can and can't give you

How to read every empirical claim in this project. Learning science does not provide a precise optimal method — a schedule, a single best technique, or a guaranteed route to mastery. It provides a small set of robust levers worth building defaults around, a frame for why intuitions about learning mislead, a list of folklore to avoid, and realistic limits on the hard parts. Read for that, it is useful. Read it expecting a formula, and the result is either disappointment or being misled by someone selling one.

## What it provides

1. **High-utility retention techniques.** [[The testing effect|Retrieval practice]] and [[The spacing effect|spacing]] receive high-utility ratings in Dunlosky et al. 2013. This supports using them as retention defaults; the review does not establish what proportion of all possible learning gains they capture.
2. **A frame for why intuitions mislead.** [[The metacognitive trap]] explains why familiarity can be mistaken for learning, making objective checks valuable. Their practical value depends on whether they lead to better study decisions.
3. **A list of what to stop doing.** The [[01 - Projects/Learning/Folklore catalogue|Folklore catalogue]] — learning styles, the learning pyramid, cramming-for-retention, rereading-only — is negative knowledge: practices that are reliably bad or empty, which can simply be dropped.
4. **Realistic limits on the hard parts.** Far [[Transfer of learning|transfer]] is rare and fragile; skill in low-validity domains such as sales and strategy does not come from accumulated experience the way folklore claims (see [[Deliberate practice in fuzzy domains]]). The contribution here is a warning rather than a method.

## What it does not provide

- **A precise optimal schedule.** The spacing function is broad and forgiving; there is no magic interval. The margins are not worth over-optimising.
- **A single best method per situation.** Techniques have boundary conditions — interleaving is narrow, worked examples reverse with expertise, self-explanation competes with provision. The right move depends on the material and the learner's current level.
- **A guarantee that any of it transfers to high-value skills.** Evidence for retention, comprehension, procedural skill, and professional judgment concerns different outcomes. Results for one cannot simply be assumed to establish the others; nor does that make factual knowledge unimportant for judgment.

## The effect-size reality

An effect size describes a comparison, population, outcome, and measurement interval. It is not a universal productivity multiplier. Numbers from different literatures cannot establish which intervention has the largest payoff for a particular learner. In particular, the utility ratings in Dunlosky et al. (2013) compare the study techniques reviewed; they do not rank every determinant of learning, including instruction and the conditions needed to begin studying.

## Strength of the evidence

- **Most certain — the fact and consolidation layer:** spacing, testing, sleep. Habits here can be built without hesitation.
- **Genuinely useful, moderate:** self-explanation, elaboration, worked examples for novices, scaffolded problem-based learning, concept-map construction.
- **Most cautionary — the judgment layer:** deliberate practice and intuition in low-validity professional domains, where the message is a warning (manufacture feedback) rather than a method.

The practical workflow also depends on starting and sustaining it. [[Designing the learning environment]], [[Starting and sustaining a learning habit]], and [[Feedback, help, and learning with others]] add relevant research with its own limitations. Evidence that a strategy changes behaviour or short-term attention is not automatically evidence that it improves delayed learning.

## The two failure modes this protects against

Same shape as the colour-domain Constraints, not rules frame:

1. **Dressing folklore in empirical language.** "You have to climb Bloom's taxonomy in order"; "match the material to your learning style"; "10,000 hours and you're an expert" — citing rules as if grounded when they are not. The fix: check whether the rule traces to a robust finding or to [[01 - Projects/Learning/Folklore catalogue|folklore]].
2. **Treating a robust constraint as optional preference.** "I just learn better by rereading" — when rereading is a known, identifiable inefficiency and the fluency it produces is the [[The metacognitive trap|illusion]], not learning. The preference framing obscures a real, fixable problem.

## How to read each claim in this project

- **Robust** → replicated, meta-analytically supported, holds in the field. Build on it.
- **Contested** → real effect, but mechanism or magnitude is disputed, or boundary conditions are sharp. Use it, knowing the edges.
- **Overstated** → a real kernel inflated by popular transmission. Keep the kernel, drop the inflation.
- **Folklore** → fabricated or refuted. Discard.

## Findings, applications, and missing evidence

A research-backed practice still requires a distinction between the tested intervention and its implementation. “If–then plans have evidence” does not establish that a particular morning routine is optimal. “Changing competing opportunities can help” does not prove a café is better than home. State the finding, cite the paper, then mark an example as an application. [[Learning evidence and open questions]] applies this standard to the expanded workflow and records what remains unresolved.
