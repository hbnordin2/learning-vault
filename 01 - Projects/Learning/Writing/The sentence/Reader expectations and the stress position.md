# Reader expectations and the stress position

Gopen and Swan's reader-expectation account of prose: readers make many of their interpretive decisions from the *structural location* of information, not only from the words. Where a piece of information sits in a sentence, relative to where readers expect that kind of information, shapes how it is read. In their words, "the resulting structure, even more than the meanings of individual words, significantly influences the reader during the act of interpretation."

Two positions in a sentence carry fixed expectations.

- **The stress position** — the end of a sentence is where readers naturally place emphasis and expect the most important, new information. "Readers naturally emphasize the material that arrives at the end of a sentence." It is where the reader "needs and expects closure and fulfilment," so the point worth emphasising belongs there, not buried mid-sentence.
- **The topic position** — the beginning establishes what the sentence is about and links back to what came before. Readers "expect a unit of discourse to be a story about whoever shows up first," and expect the opening to supply context and linkage. Old, already-known material belongs here; putting familiar information first is what lets the reader follow the logical thread.

This is the given-new contract ([[Cognitive load in prose|Clark & Haviland, 1977]]) stated as a placement rule: old information in the topic position, new information in the stress position.

## Two supporting expectations

- **Subject-verb proximity.** "Readers expect a grammatical subject to be followed immediately by the verb." Length inserted between subject and verb is read as an interruption, and it competes with the main clause for working memory. This connects to the character-and-action guidance in [[Characters and actions]].
- **One unit of discourse, one point.** "Each unit of discourse, no matter what the size, is expected to serve a single function, to make a single point" — a clause, a sentence, a section, an article. A sentence carrying two competing points forces the reader to guess which one earns the stress position.

## Evidence tier

- **The reader-expectation framework (topic and stress positions)**: a principled rhetorical and psycholinguistic argument, *not* a controlled experiment. Gopen and Swan run no comprehension study; their evidence is before-and-after rewrites of journal prose that they judge clearer. The essay's title ("The Science of Scientific Writing") invites the common overstatement that it was experimentally validated. It was not.
- **The underlying substrate is experimental.** The topic/stress rule restates the given-new contract, which *is* supported by reaction-time experiments (Haviland & Clark, 1974) — see [[Cognitive load in prose]] — and descends from the Prague-School theme-rheme tradition and Halliday. The theory rests on tested ground even though this paper tests nothing itself.
- **Practical status**: widely taught in scientific and technical writing; a strong heuristic, not a measured intervention.
