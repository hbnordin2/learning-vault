# Characters and actions

Joseph Williams's diagnosis of what makes a sentence read as clear: "readers want to see characters in your subjects and their actions in your verbs." A sentence is easy to process when its grammatical subjects name the main characters — the people or things it is about — and its verbs name what those characters do. Prose turns abstract and hard to follow when the real actions are moved out of the verb slot.

## Nominalizations

The main culprit Williams names is the **nominalization** — a noun made from a verb or an adjective: *decision* from *decide*, *failure* from *fail*, *reaction* from *react*, *analysis* from *analyse*. When the action of a sentence is packed into a nominalization, the verb slot falls to a weak stand-in ("there is," "conduct," "make," "perform"), and the actor often disappears. "There was a reduction in errors by the team" hides both the action (reduce) and the character (the team) that "the team reduced errors" puts in plain view. The repair is mechanical: find the real action and make it the verb; find the character doing it and make it the subject.

## Clarity is a reader's experience, not a sentence property

Williams grounds the guidance in the reader rather than in a rulebook: "clarity is not a property of sentences but an impression of readers." A sentence is not clear or unclear in the abstract; it is clear when a reader experiences it as clear. Character-subjects and action-verbs work because they match how readers look for who did what — the same reason the stress and topic positions work (see [[Reader expectations and the stress position]]).

## Evidence tier

- **Characters-as-subjects, actions-as-verbs, and the nominalization diagnosis**: craft-principled advice, cognitively motivated. Williams explains *why* it works in terms of reader processing, but he offers a theory, not controlled outcome studies. It converges with the given-new and subject-verb-proximity findings that *are* experimentally supported (see [[Cognitive load in prose]]), which is the source of confidence in it.
- **Lineage**: Williams (1933–2008), *Style: Toward Clarity and Grace* (1990) and *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*, continued in later editions with Joseph Bizup. Long-standing and widely adopted in university writing programs — practitioner authority, not an experimental result.
