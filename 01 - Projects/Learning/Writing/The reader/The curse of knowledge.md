# The curse of knowledge

The difficulty a knowledgeable person has in imagining what it is like for someone else not to know what they already know. Steven Pinker calls it "the single best explanation I know of why good people write bad prose": once a writer knows a subject, the missing context, the unexplained jargon, and the unstated assumptions are invisible to them, because their own mind supplies all of it automatically. The reader, lacking that knowledge, cannot.

## Where the evidence comes from

The phenomenon is well-established in behavioural science, under this name and adjacent ones.

- **Camerer, Loewenstein, and Weber (1989)** brought the term "curse of knowledge" into the research literature, with experiments showing that better-informed agents cannot ignore their private knowledge when predicting the judgments of less-informed others. (The phrase itself they credit to Robin Hogarth.)
- **Elizabeth Newton's "tappers and listeners"** study (Stanford, 1990) is the vivid demonstration: people tapping out a well-known tune predicted listeners would identify it about half the time; listeners actually managed about one in forty (3 of 120). Knowing the tune, the tappers could not hear the toneless tapping as a listener would. (The study is an unpublished dissertation, popularised by Chip and Dan Heath's *Made to Stick*; it is legitimate underlying evidence for the effect, not Pinker's own example.)
- **Nickerson (1999)** reviews the broader tendency to impute one's own knowledge to others, and the effect is a close relative of hindsight bias (Fischhoff, 1975) — in both, knowing something makes the ignorant state hard to reconstruct.

## The prescription, and its weaker footing

Pinker's remedy is **classic style**, a stance he takes from Thomas and Turner (*Clear and Simple as the Truth*): treat prose as a window onto the world, the writer showing the reader something they could see for themselves, writer and reader as equals. The practical moves that follow — name concrete things, define terms, show a draft to a real reader, reread the draft as a stranger would — all work against the writer's own missing-context blind spot. This connects to the schema-building job in [[Prior knowledge and comprehension]]: the writer has to supply, in the text, the knowledge they wrongly assume the reader already holds.

## Evidence tier

- **The curse of knowledge itself (the diagnosis)**: robust, replicated experimental literature across psychology and economics (Camerer et al., 1989; Nickerson, 1999; the hindsight-bias work). Strong.
- **The Newton figures**: widely reported and credible, but from an unpublished dissertation and always cited second-hand. Treat as illustrative.
- **Classic style as the cure (the prescription)**: persuasive, psychologically motivated craft advice from Pinker and from Thomas and Turner — not tested by controlled outcome studies. The seam: the disease is measured; the recommended treatment is principled, not trialled.
