# Prior knowledge and comprehension

Bartlett (1932) established that memory is reconstructive, not reproductive: readers distort incoming information to fit existing schemas. Comprehension depends less on the text and more on what the reader brings to it. Beyond a threshold of decoding ability, domain knowledge is the dominant predictor of reading comprehension — a position sometimes called "knowledge is comprehension."

## The Bransford and Johnson demonstration

Bransford and Johnson (1972) gave participants an ambiguous passage about washing clothes without identifying the topic. Participants who received the topic label **before** hearing the passage recalled approximately 8.0 idea units out of 14; those who received no context recalled only 3.6-3.8. Providing the same context **after** the passage produced scores barely above the no-context condition. The finding: prior knowledge must be activated before encoding to aid comprehension. Having the knowledge is not enough; it must be available during reading.

## Schema theory

Schemas are organized knowledge structures that provide a framework for interpreting new information ([[How memory works]]). When a reader encounters text about a familiar domain, the relevant schema activates and provides slots for incoming propositions, reduces the working memory burden of inference, and enables gap-filling when the text is incomplete. When the reader lacks the schema, each proposition must be processed as isolated information, which is both slower and less memorable.

Recht and Leslie (1988) showed that domain knowledge predicted reading comprehension of a baseball passage better than general reading ability. Schneider et al. (1989) replicated the pattern: low-ability readers with high domain knowledge outperformed high-ability readers with low domain knowledge. The causal direction is harder to establish — most evidence is correlational — but the convergent pattern is robust.

## The implication for writers

A writer cannot write their way out of a knowledge gap. If readers lack the prerequisite schemas, no amount of coherence or plain language will produce deep understanding — the situation model ([[Writing for comprehension|Kintsch, 1988]]) requires prior knowledge to integrate with. The writer's job in this case is to **build the schema in the text**: definitions, analogies, worked examples, and enough redundancy that the reader can construct the missing structure.

But if readers already have the schema, over-explanation can be counterproductive. The reverse coherence effect ([[Coherence in text|McNamara et al., 1996]]) shows that high-knowledge readers sometimes comprehend better when text is less explicit, because coherence gaps force inference. The practical upshot: calibrate explanation to the reader's knowledge level, and know that the same text can be too sparse for one reader and too dense for another.

## Evidence tier

- **Bransford & Johnson (1972)**: classic controlled experiment, replicated many times. Strong.
- **Domain knowledge predicts comprehension better than general ability**: convergent evidence from multiple correlational studies (Recht & Leslie, 1988; Schneider et al., 1989). Strong for the pattern; weaker on establishing causal direction.
- **"Build the schema in the text"**: principled extrapolation. Reasonable but not validated as a specific instructional intervention.
