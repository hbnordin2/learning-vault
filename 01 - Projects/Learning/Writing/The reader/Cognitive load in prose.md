# Cognitive load in prose

[[Cognitive load and worked examples|Cognitive load theory]] (Sweller, 1988) divides processing demands into intrinsic load (inherent difficulty of the material), extraneous load (imposed by how material is presented), and germane load (productive effort building schemas). The bottleneck is working memory, which holds roughly 5-9 chunks simultaneously. Applied to reading, the practical question is: which features of prose impose extraneous load — processing that consumes working memory without advancing understanding?

## What counts as extraneous load in writing

Extraneous load in prose includes anything the reader must process that does not contribute to building a situation model ([[Writing for comprehension|Kintsch, 1988]]):

- **Syntactic complexity.** Garden-path sentences (where initial parsing leads to a wrong structural interpretation) impose measurable processing costs. Readers with lower working memory capacity are disproportionately affected by non-canonical sentence structures (passive voice, centre-embedded clauses, long-distance dependencies).
- **Ambiguous reference.** Pronouns without clear antecedents force the reader to search backward, consuming working memory on resolution rather than meaning.
- **Decorative formatting.** Font clutter, poor contrast, and dense layout all impose extraneous load that competes with comprehension. Weak decoding skills amplify this: "weak decoding skills act as a bottleneck, increasing cognitive load and leaving fewer cognitive resources available for reading comprehension" (Connor et al.).
- **Unnecessary jargon.** Unfamiliar terminology forces the reader to decode at the word level, leaving fewer resources for proposition-level and situation-model processing.

## Sentence length

The American Press Institute found that at an average of 14 words per sentence, readers understand more than 90% of content; at 43 words, comprehension drops below 10%. The International Plain Language Association recommends 15-20 words on average. Morkes and Nielsen found that concisely written text produced 58% higher comprehension scores in a web-reading study.

## The given-new contract

Clark and Haviland (1977) proposed that writers implicitly contract with readers to place known ("given") information before unknown ("new") information in each sentence. Experimental evidence shows a paragraph that follows the given-new ordering is easier to process than one with the same content but reversed ordering. The mechanism: when given information has a clear antecedent in the reader's mental model, new information attaches cleanly; when it does not, the reader must construct a bridging inference, which consumes working memory.

## Evidence tier

- **Cognitive load theory framework**: established by controlled laboratory experiments (Sweller & Chandler, 1991; Sweller, 1988). Strong.
- **Application to prose style** (e.g., "convoluted syntax = extraneous load"): principled extrapolation from the theory. CLT experiments typically manipulate instructional formats (diagrams, worked examples), not prose style directly.
- **Sentence length numbers**: the American Press Institute figures are widely cited but come from a single study with limited methodological detail. Treat as practitioner evidence.
- **Given-new contract**: grounded in psycholinguistic experiments with reaction-time measures (Clark & Haviland, 1977; Haviland & Clark, 1974). Strong for the specific claim.
- **Plain language movement** (Cutts, Kimble, federal guidelines): practitioner consensus supported by case studies, not controlled comprehension experiments.
