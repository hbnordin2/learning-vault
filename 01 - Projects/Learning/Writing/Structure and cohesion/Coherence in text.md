# Coherence in text

Coherence is the degree to which ideas in a text connect to each other and to a discernible macrostructure. McNamara, Kintsch, Songer, and Kintsch (1996) operationalized it along two dimensions. **Local coherence** concerns sentence-to-sentence connections: connective words ("because," "however"), replacing pronouns with explicit noun referents, and argument overlap between adjacent sentences. **Global coherence** concerns macrostructure: topic sentences, headers, and cues that signal how sections relate to the whole.

## The reverse coherence effect

Two experiments with biology texts (McNamara et al., 1996) produced a genuinely surprising interaction:

- **Low-knowledge readers** benefited from high coherence on both dimensions. Cohesive devices helped them bridge gaps they could not fill from background knowledge.
- **High-knowledge readers** scored better on measures of deep conceptual understanding when reading *less* coherent text. Low coherence forced them to generate inferences, producing deeper integration with prior knowledge.

The reversal appeared only on **situation-model measures** (deep understanding), not on textbase measures (factual recall). High-knowledge readers recalled facts equally well regardless of coherence; their conceptual grasp improved when the text made them work. This is a form of [[Desirable difficulties|desirable difficulty]] — processing that feels harder but produces better learning.

## The reversal of the reversal

O'Reilly and McNamara (2007) found that the reverse coherence effect disappears when high-knowledge readers also use **active reading strategies**. Strategic readers extract deep understanding from coherent text too. The benefit of low coherence was partly an artifact of passive reading — coherence gaps forced an engagement that strategic readers supply voluntarily.

The implication: the reverse coherence effect is real but not a reason to write incoherent text. For a general audience, coherent text is safer. For expert readers, the comprehension risk of coherence is mitigated by active strategies (retrieval practice, self-explanation — see [[Elaboration and self-explanation]], [[The testing effect]]).

## Evidence tier

- **The coherence-knowledge interaction**: established by controlled experiments with pre/post measures (McNamara et al., 1996; McNamara, 2001). Among the strongest evidence in the comprehension-writing literature.
- **The reversal-of-reversal**: controlled experiment (O'Reilly & McNamara, 2007). Strong for its specific claim.
- **Practical recommendation** ("write for coherence, rely on testing for desirable difficulty"): principled extrapolation. Reasonable but not directly tested as a combined intervention.
