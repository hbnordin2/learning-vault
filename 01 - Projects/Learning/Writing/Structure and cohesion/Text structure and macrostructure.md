# Text structure and macrostructure

Bonnie Meyer's research on how the top-level organisation of a text affects how much of it readers understand and remember. Beyond individual sentences and their local links, a text has an overall rhetorical shape — its **macrostructure** — and readers who grasp that shape recall more.

## The five top-level structures

Meyer's taxonomy of the organising patterns an expository text can take:

- **Description** (also called collection or list) — a set of attributes or items with no strong relation among them.
- **Sequence** — items ordered in time or by procedure.
- **Comparison** — two or more things set against each other (compare and contrast).
- **Causation** — a cause-and-effect relation.
- **Problem-solution** — a problem stated, then a response to it.

## The tightly-organised structures are recalled better

Meyer and Freedle (1984) presented the same content arranged as different structures and found the more tightly related types — comparison, causation, and problem-solution — produced better recall than the same information delivered as a loose collection of descriptions. The relations in those structures give the reader a frame that holds the pieces together, where a list offers nothing to bind them.

## The structure strategy

Meyer, Brandt, and Bluth (1980) found that readers who use the author's top-level structure to organise their own recall remember more. Skilled ninth-graders did this spontaneously; low-comprehension readers did not, and making the structure explicit through [[Signaling and advance organizers|signalling]] helped them adopt it. The implication for a writer is to choose a clear organising structure and mark it, rather than leaving the reader to impose one — and, since the reader builds a macrostructure either way, to make sure the one the text offers is the one worth building.

## Evidence tier

- **The five-structure taxonomy**: Meyer's well-established framework, widely used in reading research.
- **Tightly-organised structures aid recall; the structure strategy**: a genuine, replicated experimental literature (Meyer, Brandt & Bluth, 1980; Meyer & Freedle, 1984), but old, based on free-recall paradigms with modest samples, and the original studies report effects qualitatively rather than as standardised effect sizes.
- **Modern effect size**: the meta-analysis of text-structure instruction (Bogaerds-Hazenberg, Evers-Vermeul & van den Bergh, 2021; 44 studies, grades 4–6) finds positive but modest immediate effects (Hedges' *g* ≈ 0.25 on comprehension questions, ≈ 0.37 on recall, ≈ 0.57 on summarisation) that did *not* survive to delayed post-tests. The honest reading: structure helps comprehension modestly and in the moment, not massively or durably.
