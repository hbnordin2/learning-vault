# Writing for comprehension

Comprehension is not a property of the text alone. Kintsch's construction-integration model (1988) identifies three levels of text representation: **surface structure** (exact wording, retained for seconds), **textbase** (the propositions explicitly stated), and **situation model** (the reader's integrated understanding, combining text with prior knowledge). Deep comprehension means building the situation model, and whether that happens depends on the interaction between what the text does and what the reader already knows.

This folder collects the research on what makes that interaction succeed — the cognitive science of writing to be understood, held to the same [[Learning|empirical posture]] as the rest of the learning project: lean on the experimental literature, treat folk writing-advice with suspicion, and mark where the evidence is thin or the popular rendering overstates.

## The folder, by sub-cluster

**The reader** — the reader's machinery, and the writer's flawed model of it.
- [[Cognitive load in prose]] — working memory is the bottleneck; convoluted syntax, ambiguous reference, and clutter impose extraneous load that competes with meaning.
- [[Prior knowledge and comprehension]] — beyond a decoding threshold, domain knowledge is the dominant predictor of comprehension; no prose clarity compensates for a missing schema.
- [[The curse of knowledge]] — the writer cannot see the context, jargon, and assumptions their own knowledge supplies; the reader cannot supply them either.

**The sentence** — clarity locally.
- [[Characters and actions]] — Williams: put the characters in the subjects and their actions in the verbs; nominalizations bury the action.
- [[Reader expectations and the stress position]] — Gopen & Swan: old information in the topic position, new information in the stress position; keep subjects next to verbs.

**Structure and cohesion** — connecting ideas.
- [[Coherence in text]] — local and global coherence; the reverse coherence effect for expert readers.
- [[Text structure and macrostructure]] — Meyer: the top-level rhetorical shape readers use to organise recall.
- [[Signaling and advance organizers]] — headings and cues that help readers build the macrostructure.

**Style** — choices about vividness and voice.
- [[Concrete language and seductive details]] — concreteness aids encoding but can bind understanding to surface features; vivid-but-irrelevant detail harms outright.
- [[Personalization and conversational style]] — Mayer: conversational, direct address helps novices learn, with boundary conditions that keep expert reference prose in a neutral voice.

## What the findings converge on

- Anything the reader must process that does not advance the situation model reduces comprehension — [[Cognitive load in prose|cognitive load]] applied to prose.
- The connections between ideas matter more than the complexity of single sentences. Cohesion helps low-knowledge readers; for high-knowledge readers, some coherence gaps deepen understanding by forcing inference — see [[Coherence in text]].
- Concrete language aids initial comprehension but can impair transfer, and vivid irrelevant detail harms outright — see [[Concrete language and seductive details]].
- Domain knowledge dominates once decoding is fluent; for a novice reader the writer's job is to build the missing schema in the text — see [[Prior knowledge and comprehension]].
- Structural signals and a clear top-level structure help readers who would not build a macrostructure on their own, though the measured effects are more modest than practitioner advice implies — see [[Signaling and advance organizers]], [[Text structure and macrostructure]].

## Readability formulas

Traditional readability formulas (Flesch-Kincaid, Dale-Chall) use surface proxies — sentence length, word length — and miss nearly everything above. A highly cohesive text can score as *less* readable because connective phrases add words and length. Coh-Metrix (Graesser, McNamara et al., 2004), which measures referential cohesion, semantic overlap, and discourse structure, significantly outperforms Flesch-Kincaid at predicting actual comprehension (Crossley et al., 2011). The strong claim — that readability formulas are unreliable for predicting individual-text comprehension — is well-supported. They retain some utility for comparing large corpora at coarse grain.

## The writer's core tradeoff

Making text maximally coherent and explicit helps novice readers but can make expert readers passive. The reverse coherence effect ([[Coherence in text|McNamara et al., 1996]]) shows high-knowledge readers scoring better on deep-understanding measures when text is *less* coherent, because the gaps force them to generate inferences. The same pattern recurs across the folder — personalization, signalling, and concreteness all help novices most and can reverse for experts (the expertise-reversal effect). The practical resolution: write for coherence by default for a general audience, and for expert readers rely on retrieval practice and testing to supply the generation demand that explicit text removes — see [[The testing effect]], [[Desirable difficulties]].

## Evidence posture

Most effects here are medium, not transformative, and the popular writing books inflate them. The robust, experimentally-grounded results are the cognitive-load and given-new findings, the coherence-knowledge interaction, the concreteness advantage and the seductive-details effect, and the curse of knowledge. The reader-expectation and character-action frameworks (Gopen & Swan, Williams) are principled craft grounded in that experimental substrate but not themselves trialled. Signalling, advance organizers, and text-structure instruction show real but modest and often short-lived effects. Each note carries an explicit evidence tier, so the seam between tested finding, principled extrapolation, and practitioner consensus stays visible.
