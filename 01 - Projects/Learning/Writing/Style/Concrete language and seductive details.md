# Concrete language and seductive details

Paivio's dual coding theory (1971, 1986) proposes two representational systems: verbal and imagistic. Concrete words ("lobster," "bicycle") activate both systems; abstract words ("justice," "entropy") primarily activate verbal coding alone. The additive effect of dual encoding produces a robust **concreteness advantage** in recall and recognition — one of the most replicated findings in memory research, established across hundreds of experiments. See [[Dual coding and multimedia learning]].

For writers, the implication seems straightforward: prefer concrete language. But two boundary conditions complicate this.

## Seductive details

Harp and Mayer (1998) showed that adding vivid, concrete but **irrelevant** anecdotes to instructional text about lightning formation produced worse recall and worse comprehension. The mechanism is primarily diversion: readers construct mental models around the interesting detail rather than the target content. When participants were told the details were "not relevant to the learning objective," the effect disappeared — confirming it operates through perceived relevance, not mere distraction.

The seductive details effect is well-established across meta-analyses spanning 35 years. The practical rule: concrete examples must be **structurally relevant** to the principle being taught. An example that illustrates the target concept is helpful; an example that is vivid but tangential is actively harmful.

## The concreteness-transfer tradeoff

Fyfe, McNeil, and Borjas (2015) found across two experiments that richer contextual concreteness in instructional examples helped immediate comprehension but **harmed transfer**. In Experiment 1 (N = 1,066), high-immersion training produced 0.31 improvement on transfer items vs. 0.40 for low-immersion (p = 0.047). In Experiment 2 (N = 144), low-context learners improved 1.58 points on transfer while high-context learners showed no improvement (-0.36). The mechanism: concrete details cause learners to represent principles in "a less abstract and generalizable form," binding understanding to surface features — see [[Transfer of learning]].

## The resolution

The concreteness advantage and the concreteness-transfer tradeoff are not contradictory. They operate at different levels:

- Concrete language aids **initial encoding** — the concept enters memory more strongly.
- Concrete context can impair **abstraction** — the concept binds to its example rather than becoming a transferable principle.

The practical resolution: use concrete examples to anchor understanding, but ensure the example maps onto the target structure and is not the only instance the reader encounters. Multiple structurally-varied examples preserve the concreteness advantage while enabling the reader to extract the underlying principle — see [[Elaboration and self-explanation]].

## Evidence tier

- **Concreteness advantage in recall**: controlled experiments, hundreds of replications. Very strong.
- **Seductive details effect**: meta-analyses across 35+ years. Strong.
- **Concreteness-transfer tradeoff**: controlled experiments (Fyfe et al., 2015), fewer replications. Moderate — a strong signal but not yet a settled law.
- **"Use multiple structurally-varied examples"**: principled extrapolation from the above. Not directly tested as a combined writing recommendation.
