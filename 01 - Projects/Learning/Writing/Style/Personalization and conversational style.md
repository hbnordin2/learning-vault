# Personalization and conversational style

Richard Mayer's **personalization principle**: people learn more from instructional material written in a conversational style — including first- and second-person address ("you," "your," "I," "we") — than from the same material in a formal, impersonal third person. It is one of the social-cue principles in his cognitive theory of multimedia learning.

## The finding

- **Moreno and Mayer (2000)** ran five experiments (a lightning lesson, an environmental-science game) comparing personalized wording against neutral wording; the personalized versions produced better performance on transfer and problem-solving tests.
- **Mayer, Fennell, Farmer, and Campbell (2004)** changed "the" to "your" in twelve places of a narrated biology lesson and found better transfer — but not better retention, and the benefit was concentrated in low-prior-knowledge learners.
- **The mechanism is social agency** (Mayer, Sobko, and Mautone, 2003): conversational cues prime the learner to treat the source as a conversational partner and to engage in deeper, generative processing, as if in a dialogue rather than reading a document.

## The boundary conditions matter more than the headline

The effect is real but frequently overstated. Mayer's often-quoted figure — a median *d* of 1.11 across eleven of his own short studies — is an author's vote-count of small lab experiments, not a corrected pooled estimate. The independent meta-analysis (Ginns, Martin, and Marsh, 2013) gives a truer picture: transfer *d* ≈ 0.54 and retention *d* ≈ 0.30, with self-reported interest and perceived helpfulness not reliably improved. The effect concentrates where it can help and fades where it cannot:

- It helps **novices** far more than experts, and can reverse for experts — an instance of the expertise-reversal effect.
- It **fades in longer material** — small and non-significant for instruction over about 35 minutes; most positive results come from lessons under ten minutes.
- The gain is mostly in **transfer, not verbatim retention**, and over-personalising or laying on politeness washes it out (and what reads as polite is culture- and language-dependent).
- Nearly all the evidence is short, narrated instructional multimedia delivered to students. It has been shown in written text too (Kartal, 2011), but there is essentially no evidence for expert reference reading, and a recent study (Almeida et al., 2024) failed to find the benefit.

## Why this does not push the vault's notes to "you"

Because the principle is the strongest evidence for conversational, second-person address, it is worth stating plainly what it does and does not license. It applies to *instructional material aimed at novices*, works through social agency, and helps most in short lessons where the reader is being walked through something new. Standalone reference prose for a knowledgeable reader is the opposite case on every axis — the expert-reader, [[Coherence in text|reverse-coherence]] regime where extra hand-holding gives flat-to-negative returns. So a neutral third-person voice in reference notes is not in tension with the evidence; the personalization finding governs a different genre. Where it *would* apply: an explicitly instructional, novice-facing how-to, where conversational direct address is the evidence-backed choice.

## Evidence tier

- **The personalization principle (conversational style aids learning)**: multiple controlled experiments plus a meta-analysis. Strong for its scope — but the honest effect size is moderate (transfer *d* ≈ 0.5), not the inflated *d* ≈ 1.1.
- **Boundary conditions** (novices > experts, fades in long lessons, transfer > retention): established by the meta-analysis and primary studies. Strong.
- **Extending it to expert reference prose**: unsupported; principled extrapolation at best, with at least one null result.
