# Sleep and consolidation

Sleep matters before and after learning. The research supports protecting sleep around study, while leaving substantial uncertainty about precise timing and the size of a benefit for an individual learner.

## Sleep consolidates memory — robust

Sleep supports memory consolidation through processes reviewed by Rasch & Born (2013), including reactivation and reorganisation. Effects depend on the memory task and sleep conditions; assigning all declarative memory to one stage and all skill memory to another is too simple. [Review](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.00032.2012).

Sleep before learning also matters. Yoo et al. (2007) found poorer memory formation following a night of total sleep deprivation. The often-quoted percentage reduction belongs to that experiment; it is not a general formula for every tired learner or each hour of lost sleep. [Experiment](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn1851).

The practical consequences:

- **Application:** protect sleep before and after substantial learning instead of assuming extra waking study time is a free gain.
- **Application:** distribute practice across occasions rather than relying on an all-nighter; this combines the sleep evidence with [[The spacing effect]].
- The evidence does **not** establish that everyone should study their hardest material immediately before bed, especially if that displaces sleep. No universal bedtime study window is prescribed here.

## Spacing across sleep

Spacing and sleep are related but distinct literatures. Spacing benefits are not restricted to gaps containing sleep; see [[The spacing effect]]. Sessions across days provide both separated practice and opportunities for sleep, but the two benefits cannot simply be added or treated as one mechanism.

## Exercise

Erickson et al. (2011) studied a year-long aerobic-exercise intervention in 120 **older adults**, finding increased anterior hippocampal volume and improved spatial memory. Associations with serum BDNF do not by themselves prove causal mediation. This study is not a direct estimate of the memory benefit for a healthy adult in their thirties, and cannot establish an optimum exercise schedule around studying. [Paper](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1015950108).

Exercise programming belongs in Exercise. The specific claim that a particular post-study exercise window reliably locks in learning is not prescribed here.
