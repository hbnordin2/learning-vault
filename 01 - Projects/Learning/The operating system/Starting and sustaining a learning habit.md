# Starting and sustaining a learning habit

Intending to learn and having a plan for starting are different. **Implementation intentions** specify a response to a recognisable situation: if this happens, then do that. They are researched planning interventions, not a guarantee of discipline.

## Specify the cue and the action

Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer's meta-analysis of 642 tests found benefits from implementation intentions across different outcomes and populations. Its unadjusted average effect was approximately *d* = 0.36; a robust publication-bias adjustment reduced the estimate to approximately 0.15. The direction supports if–then planning, while the sensitivity of the magnitude argues against advertising it as a dramatic transformation. This is broad self-regulation evidence, not 642 trials of adult studying. [Paper, published online 2024; journal issue 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/10463283.2024.2334563).

**Application:** replace “learn databases this week” with “after lunch on Tuesday, at the desk, open the query exercise and attempt its first question.” The cue, location, and response are explicit. This exact wording and schedule are illustrations, not a tested optimum.

The planned action still needs to fit the learner's knowledge. If the first question is incomprehensible, the next action can be studying an explained example; see [[Cognitive load and worked examples]]. Planning when to begin does not replace instruction in how to do the work.

## What habit formation actually establishes

Lally et al. (2010) followed adults repeating a chosen eating, drinking, or activity behaviour in a consistent context over 12 weeks. Reported automaticity generally increased with repetition. Estimated times to approach an individual's automaticity plateau varied widely; some estimates extended beyond the observation period. The familiar “66 days” is not a universal deadline. [Paper](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674).

This was an observational study of relatively simple health behaviours, with self-reported automaticity. It does not establish how long learning a subject takes, or that demanding intellectual work becomes effortless.

**Application:** repeat the start of a learning session after an identifiable event. The intended habit is getting into the work; choosing an argument, understanding a mechanism, and correcting an error still require thought. That distinction prevents “automatic studying” from becoming a misleading promise.

## A missed occasion is not evidence of a reset

In Lally et al.'s data, missing one opportunity did not materially disrupt habit development. That supports rejecting the claim that one missed day necessarily erases progress. It does not establish an exact permissible number of misses, or a “never miss twice” rule.

**Application:** resume at the next specified cue and record whether it actually happened. The purpose is to restore the behaviour and inspect the plan, not to preserve an allegedly scientific streak. Monitoring is covered separately in [[Planning and monitoring learning]].

## What is not claimed

Neither paper establishes a universal two-minute starting rule, a fixed number of days to build a habit, or the claim that motivation becomes irrelevant. For why a task is avoided even when the plan is clear, see [[Motivation, avoidance, and difficulty]].
