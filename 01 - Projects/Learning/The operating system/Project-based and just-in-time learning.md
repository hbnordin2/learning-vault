# Project-based and just-in-time learning

Learning embedded in a real problem: take on an actual project, pull in knowledge when a wall is reached, and apply it immediately. For a knowledgeable adult this is often the optimal mode rather than a compromise. The one substantive caveat concerns genuine novices, and it needs stating precisely.

## What the evidence says

Problem-based learning (PBL) has a consistent, moderate signature across meta-analyses (Dochy et al., 2003; Gijbels et al., 2005; Walker & Leary, 2009): PBL students gain slightly less raw factual knowledge but show better application, transfer, retention, and self-directed-learning skill. Dochy found a skills effect around +0.46 with knowledge slightly negative and retention better; Gijbels found PBL's advantage is largest at the "principle-linking" level — understanding how concepts connect and when to apply them. That is the level most relevant to applied work: not reciting the definition of a go-to-market motion, but knowing which one fits a given situation.

## The novice caveat, stated precisely

The counterweight is the [[Direct instruction vs discovery learning|minimal-guidance]] result (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006): for a novice with no schema, an open-ended problem overloads working memory on search, so little gets encoded — worked examples and scaffolding beat unguided problem-solving. This advantage recedes as prior knowledge rises (the [[Cognitive load and worked examples|expertise-reversal effect]]), and well-designed PBL is heavily scaffolded anyway, so the critique applies to unguided discovery rather than to scaffolded projects. Both sides agree the moderator is prior knowledge.

## When this mode applies, and the exception

Adjacent experience can be relevant, but the guidance literature does not establish that an experienced engineer or founder is already knowledgeable across most neighbouring domains. The practical application is to check the required knowledge and execution on a representative task. A scaffolded project may be appropriate where the prerequisites are available; otherwise add instruction. The campaign-with-a-playbook example illustrates this approach, rather than establishing a generally optimal way to learn go-to-market.

The exception is a genuinely new sub-domain where no schema exists — the mechanics of a specific ad platform, enterprise contract structure, an unfamiliar regulatory regime. There, revert to explicit instruction first: a worked example, a template, a 90-minute expert walkthrough, then switch to live practice once one correct instance can be executed. Do not attempt to discover fundamentals with no foothold in them.

## Avoiding "just-once" learning

A real risk of project-driven learning is solving the immediate problem and never extracting the reusable pattern, so every instance starts from scratch. Counter it by performing the generative step after the project: write the reusable principle, the template, and what to do differently next time, so the one-off becomes a [[Expertise, schemas, and chunking|schema]] carried forward. Just-in-time learning gets work moving; the after-action extraction is what turns doing into learning.

## The branding is not the evidence

"Just-in-time learning" and andragogy (Knowles's adult-learning theory) are popular wrappers around this. Knowles himself called andragogy "a model of assumptions," not a theory, and its assumptions — that adults learn differently because they are adults — are largely untested and partly self-refuted: self-direction varies more by individual and domain familiarity than by age (see [[Metacognition and self-regulated learning]] on the thin support). The cited problem-based-learning studies support particular instructional designs and outcomes. They do not establish that every knowledgeable adult learns best by pulling information only at the point of need. The brand names are not the evidence; the mechanism is.
