# Learning from worksheets

A worksheet is a set of questions answered without the source in view, with the correct answer and its explanation revealed only after each attempt. Built well, it is [[The testing effect|retrieval practice]] with feedback, which is among the best-supported ways to learn. Built badly, it is a recognition exercise that feels like learning and leaves little behind ([[The metacognitive trap]]). This note sets out what the research says about building one well. The `/worksheet` skill turns it into a procedure, and the filled-in worksheets live in `01 - Projects/Learning/Worksheets/`.

The mechanisms are covered elsewhere in this project. What this note adds is the item-level layer: which question formats to use, how to write multiple-choice options, what feedback to give, how to mix question types, and how one worksheet should use the results of the last.

## Multiple choice works, if the wrong options are good

Laboratory studies favour recall over recognition: producing an answer builds more durable memory than picking it from a list ([[Retrieval is reconstruction]]). The classroom evidence is more forgiving. Yang et al. (2021) pooled 222 classroom studies with over 48,000 students and found that quiz format did not significantly change the benefit. Multiple-choice quizzes produced g ≈ 0.57, short-answer g ≈ 0.64 and free recall g ≈ 0.24, and the differences between formats were not statistically significant. Multiple choice is therefore a sound format for a worksheet, provided the options make the learner retrieve.

That proviso is about the wrong options, called distractors or lures. Little, Bjork, Bjork & Angello (2012) found that multiple-choice questions whose wrong options were plausible and competitive matched cued-recall questions on the tested facts. They also improved later recall of facts linked to the wrong options, which cued recall did not. The reason is that ruling out a plausible option requires retrieving why it is wrong. When the wrong options are obviously wrong, that benefit disappears, because the learner can choose by elimination without retrieving anything.

Multiple choice carries one specific risk. Roediger & Marsh (2005) found that reading wrong options can plant them: learners who picked a lure later produced it as an answer, and more lures meant more of this. Feedback largely removes the problem. Butler & Roediger (2008) showed that feedback after a multiple-choice test raised correct answers and cut lure intrusions on a later test.

On the number of options, Rodriguez (2005), a meta-analysis of 80 years of test research, concluded that three options are best in most settings. Writers rarely manage more than two plausible wrong answers, and a fourth or fifth option is usually filler that the learner rules out at a glance. That study measured test quality, not learning, but it fits the lure findings: fewer, better wrong options do more work and plant fewer errors.

## Every answer gets feedback, and the feedback explains

Feedback raises the classroom testing effect from g ≈ 0.37 to g ≈ 0.54 (Yang et al., 2021). It matters most when an answer is wrong, because retrieving an error without correction strengthens the error ([[Retrieval is reconstruction]]).

The content of the feedback matters for transfer. Butler, Godbole & Marsh (2013) gave learners either the correct answer or the correct answer with an explanation. Both did equally well when the same questions came back two days later. Only the explanation group did better on new questions that required applying the idea. Feedback should therefore say why the right answer is right, and for multiple choice, why each wrong option is wrong. The second part extends the Little et al. finding: the learner learns about the ideas behind the lures as well as the answer.

On timing, laboratory work with multiple-choice tests found that delayed feedback beat immediate feedback on later tests (Butler, Karpicke & Roediger, 2007), while classroom studies are mixed (Kulik & Kulik, 1988). A self-study worksheet reveals feedback as soon as each item is answered, which is the practical choice. The spacing benefit of delay comes instead from re-testing missed items in a later worksheet.

## Confident errors are the most valuable errors

When a learner gets an answer wrong while feeling sure of it, the correction is more likely to stick than when the learner was unsure. This is the hypercorrection effect (Butterfield & Metcalfe, 2001), replicated many times in adults and children. A probable reason is that a confident error makes the correction surprising, which draws attention to it. A worksheet can use this cheaply: the learner marks confidence before seeing the answer. The mark also records calibration, which shows over time whether the feeling of knowing is trustworthy for that topic.

## Mix fact questions with application questions

Fact questions alone do not build the ability to apply ideas. Agarwal (2019) compared quizzes of fact questions, higher-order questions (application, analysis, evaluation), and a mix. Fact quizzes improved later fact tests but not higher-order tests. Higher-order and mixed quizzes improved higher-order tests, and mixed quizzes did best overall. Separately, Yang et al. (2021) found that testing benefits untested material, but much less than tested material (g ≈ 0.32 against 0.51), and Pan & Rickard (2018) found transfer real but limited to close variants of what was practised ([[Transfer of learning]]). Together these say that a worksheet improves what it asks about. If the goal is to apply an idea, the worksheet has to ask for applications, in new situations, not only for definitions.

[[Bloom's taxonomy]] is useful here only as a checklist that the question types cover more than recall. It is not a ladder that must be climbed in order.

## Mix confusable ideas

When two ideas are easy to confuse, questions that require telling them apart train that discrimination, which questions on one idea at a time do not ([[Interleaving]]). The benefit is strongest when the ideas are similar (Brunmair & Richter, 2019). A worksheet should place confusable concepts next to each other, including concepts from earlier worksheets, and should not interleave ideas that are already clearly distinct.

## Ask before teaching, when the material is new

Questions asked before studying, called prequestions or pretests, improve learning of the material they ask about, even when the first answers are wrong, provided the learner then studies the answers (Pan & Carpenter, 2023). The benefit is largest for the material the questions covered. A worksheet made before reading a source can therefore prime the reading. It is a different object from a worksheet after reading: its purpose is to direct attention, and a low score is expected.

## Novices need examples before problems

For a learner new to a procedure, studying a worked example teaches more than solving the problem unaided, and the advantage reverses as expertise grows ([[Cognitive load and worked examples]]). For procedural material, a worksheet can fade the support: a full worked example, then a partly completed one to finish, then a problem to solve alone. How far to fade depends on what earlier worksheets show the learner can already do.

## Difficulty should be effortful but passable

Retrieval helps most when it is hard but succeeds. A test so hard that nearly everything fails teaches little, and one so easy that answers are automatic teaches little ([[Desirable difficulties]]). No study fixes a target success rate for a worksheet. The usable rule is relative: if the last worksheet on a topic was near-perfect, the next should move toward application and discrimination; if it was mostly wrong, the next should add worked examples and smaller steps.

## One worksheet is not enough: re-test what was missed

Learning keeps improving with the number of times material is tested (Yang et al., 2021). Successive relearning (Rawson & Dunlosky, 2022) turns this into a procedure: practise each item until it is answered correctly, then return in later, spaced sessions and do it again. In one study it reports, recalling items once in each of three spaced sessions beat recalling them three times in one session by a wide margin at a one-week test. Spacing across sessions matters more than repetition within one ([[The spacing effect]]). For worksheets, this means each new worksheet on a topic opens by re-testing the items that the earlier worksheets show were missed or answered with low confidence. The re-test should use new wording or a new scenario, so that the learner retrieves the idea and does not recognize the old question.

## Item-writing rules

Educational measurement has a standard set of rules for writing multiple-choice questions (Haladyna, Downing & Rodriguez, 2002, 31 guidelines). They rest on expert consensus more than experiment, but they encode the same aim as the research above: the item should test the idea, not test-taking skill. The ones that matter most for a worksheet:

- The stem (the question) states one problem completely, so it could be answered before the options are read.
- Wrong options are plausible, ideally the errors a learner who half-knows the idea would make.
- Options are similar in length, grammar and detail, so the right answer does not stand out.
- No "all of the above" or "none of the above". Negative stems ("which is NOT") only when unavoidable, with the NOT emphasised.
- No clues in the stem that point to the answer, such as a word repeated from the right option.

## What this does not settle

The component findings are strong: retrieval with feedback, mixed question levels, confident-error correction, and spaced re-testing each have experimental or meta-analytic support. The worksheet as a whole is a synthesis of them and has not been tested as a package. Item counts and session length have no direct evidence and are set by what fits one sitting. Most of the multiple-choice research used prose passages and general-knowledge questions, not technical or professional material.
