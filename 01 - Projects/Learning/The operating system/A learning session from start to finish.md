# A learning session from start to finish

An applied route through the research for a self-directed adult. Each step links to its evidence and qualifications. **The sequence is a synthesis of supported components, not a protocol tested as a whole.** The examples make the steps explicit; they are not additional scientific findings.

## Before starting

1. **Define what will be attempted.** For example: explain a statistical result, solve a representative problem, or implement a small feature unaided. Record the attempt and what needs work. See [[Planning and monitoring learning]] and [Harkin et al. (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000025).
2. **Change the identified source of distraction.** If messages interrupt the session, silence them; if the home setup repeatedly invites another activity, modify the setup or choose a setting where that activity is less available. See [[Designing the learning environment]] and [Duckworth et al. (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000062). The evidence does not establish that leaving home is always better.
3. **Specify the start.** An if–then plan states the occasion and response: “After lunch, at the desk, open the exercise and attempt the first question.” See [[Starting and sustaining a learning habit]] and [Sheeran et al. (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1080/10463283.2024.2334563).

## During the session

4. **Match guidance to current knowledge.** Use an explained example when the method is unfamiliar; practise with less help as it becomes usable. See [[Cognitive load and worked examples]] and [Kalyuga et al. (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4).
5. **Produce an answer and check it.** Close the source and retrieve, explain, or attempt the relevant task; compare with reliable instruction or feedback. Classroom retrieval research supports retrieval practice, while feedback research supports attention to the information a correction provides. See [Agarwal et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-021-09595-9) and [[Feedback, help, and learning with others]]. Neither requires an arbitrary period of unaided frustration.
6. **Handle interruption and fatigue deliberately.** A brief return plan has experimental support in interruption research; short breaks have stronger evidence for reducing fatigue than improving performance. See [[Attention, interruptions, and breaks]]. There is no universal timer prescribed here.

## After the session

7. **Record what remains to be learned and arrange another attempt.** Check later performance, not just immediate familiarity. [[Planning and monitoring learning]] and [[The spacing effect]] explain the monitoring and distributed-practice evidence.
8. **Protect sleep around learning.** See [[Sleep and consolidation]] and [Rasch & Born (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.00032.2012). Sleep research does not make late-night study obligatory.

For a concrete application, someone learning SQL might specify one query to attempt, remove message notifications, study a join example if needed, write the query independently, check the result and reasoning, and attempt a related query later. The query and schedule are illustrative choices; the linked papers support the learning operations.

## If the session repeatedly fails to happen

Follow the evidence relevant to the obstacle: competing activities → [[Designing the learning environment]]; no specified occasion → [[Starting and sustaining a learning habit]]; aversion or low value → [[Motivation, avoidance, and difficulty]]; missing knowledge → [[Feedback, help, and learning with others]]. This routing is an explanatory synthesis, not a validated diagnostic instrument.
