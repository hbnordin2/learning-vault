# Learning from a book or course

The problem this solves: a book read and understood while reading then fades — the material is left behind and almost nothing usable is retained. That is the default outcome, and it fails for a specific reason: reading-then-highlighting relies on fluency, which feels like learning and is not ([[The metacognitive trap]]). The fix is to make reading generative and spaced — to retrieve and self-explain while reading, not only to receive.

## The protocol

A synthesis of retrieval, self-explanation, and spacing findings. The components have evidence; this exact sequence, reading-unit size, and calendar have not been validated as a package:

1. **Turn headings into questions before reading.** Skim the structure and convert each section heading into a question the reading is now meant to answer. This gives the subsequent reading a question to answer; it is an application, not evidence that SQ3R as a package is uniquely validated.
2. **Interrogate each major claim while reading.** Ask why the claim would be true ([[Elaboration and self-explanation|elaborative interrogation]]) and how it connects to existing knowledge and to a concrete situation (self-explanation). The applied version is to ask how the claim changes what to do next. Understanding is built here.
3. **At the end of each chapter, close the book and free-recall.** Write the three to five key claims and their reasons from memory, then reopen and patch what was missed. The struggle to reconstruct is the point — this is [[The testing effect|retrieval practice]] doing both the learning and the honest diagnosis of what was actually retained.
4. **Attempt recall again on later occasions**, then check the notes — for example after a day, a week, and a month, with the gaps adjusted to recall and the retention goal. Those intervals illustrate spacing rather than a universal optimum. Alternatively, put suitable facts into [[Spaced repetition systems|Anki]]. This is [[The spacing effect|spacing]]; without it, even good first-pass learning decays.
5. **Do not make highlighting or rereading the method.** They are the low-utility default that feels productive. Highlight to mark what to come back and retrieve, not as the studying itself.

Before this protocol: [[Designing the learning environment]] and [[Starting and sustaining a learning habit]]. If the material cannot yet be understood, use [[Cognitive load and worked examples|instruction and examples]] and [[Feedback, help, and learning with others|seek clarification]]; retrieval practice is not a requirement to invent missing fundamentals.

## Note-taking: the lever is not the tool

The "pen is mightier than the keyboard" finding (handwriting beats laptop notes; Mueller & Oppenheimer, 2014) largely failed to replicate (Morehead et al., 2019); the modality is probably not the lever. The lever the original study conflated with handwriting is real and tool-independent: reframe and compress in one's own words rather than transcribing verbatim. Generative notes (a restatement, an original structure) beat a faithful transcript, whether typed or handwritten. Take fewer, more digested notes; the highest-value note is the from-memory recall after closing the book, not the running transcript during.

## Synthesis: concept maps for connection-heavy material

When a topic is about how things relate (a market's incentive structure, the moving parts of a sales motion), build a concept map from memory — boxes for concepts, labelled arrows for relationships ("causes", "is a precondition for", "trades off against") — then check it against the source. This is the best-validated synthesis practice here: Schroeder et al. (2018) found an overall g ≈ 0.58, and constructing a map oneself (g ≈ 0.72) clearly beats studying a pre-made one (g ≈ 0.43), because construction fuses retrieval and elaboration. A note system like Zettelkasten is fine as an idea-development and output engine, but not as a validated memory tool; filing a tidy note is not the same as retaining it.

## The mindset

A book's value is the few claims that can later be retrieved and applied, not the experience of having read it cover to cover. Deeply learning and retaining a handful of important ideas from a book is worth more than reading every page fluently. Read for what changes actions, self-explain each idea against a concrete situation, retrieve them without the book, and space the review.
