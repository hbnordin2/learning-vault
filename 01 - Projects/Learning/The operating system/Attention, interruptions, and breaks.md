# Attention, interruptions, and breaks

Uninterrupted effort, switching tasks, and taking a break are different conditions. The evidence does not justify treating every minute away from a task as failure, or every pause as beneficial.

## Switching demanding tasks has costs

Rubinstein, Meyer & Evans (2001) found time costs when participants switched between tasks, with costs affected by task complexity and cues. This supports reducing unnecessary switches between demanding activities. It does not mean humans cannot perform any two activities together, nor does it establish a fixed percentage of productivity lost. [Paper](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-1523.27.4.763).

**Application:** while learning a new programming concept, keep the exercise and relevant documentation available, and defer unrelated email or messaging. Switching between documentation and code to solve the same problem is not equivalent to alternating that problem with an unrelated conversation.

## Prepare for an unavoidable interruption

Leroy & Glomb (2018) studied attention remaining on unfinished work when people anticipated returning under time pressure. Briefly planning the return reduced that residue and protected performance on the **interrupting task** across their studies. This is narrower than the popular claim that a restart note universally restores deep focus or saves a fixed number of minutes. [Paper](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2017.1184).

**Application:** before leaving an unfinished analysis for a meeting, write where the analysis stands and what will happen on return: “Query runs; next inspect duplicate customer IDs before interpreting the totals.” Using such a note at the end of a study session is an extrapolation of the same planning procedure, not a separately demonstrated retention effect.

## Breaks have better evidence for fatigue than performance

Albulescu et al. (2022) synthesised 22 independent samples of short breaks, involving 2,335 participants. Breaks improved reported vigour and reduced fatigue, with small average effects. The pooled performance improvement was not statistically significant; results differed by task. [Meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0272460).

**Application:** allow breaks to manage fatigue without presenting them as a guaranteed learning accelerator. This review does not validate a universal 25/5 or 90-minute study cycle, an optimum break activity, or an exact point at which every learner must stop. Its outcomes should not be relabelled as durable learning gains.

## Keep useful difficulty and remove interference

[[01 - Projects/Learning/Foundations/Desirable difficulties|Desirable difficulties]] concerns conditions such as retrieval and spacing. [[Cognitive load and worked examples]] concerns limits on processing and the value of guidance. Neither makes distraction a desirable difficulty. The synthesis for practice is to preserve the effort of explaining or solving while reducing unrelated demands; this is an application of those separate literatures, not a claim that all effort has the same effect.
