# Feedback, help, and learning with others

Working independently need not mean learning without instruction or correction. The relevant question is what information an interaction provides and whether the learner can subsequently use it.

## Feedback is not one uniform treatment

Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie (2020) synthesised 435 studies of educational feedback. Their main estimate after excluding extreme effect sizes was *d* ≈ 0.48, with substantial variation. Information content was an important moderator. An average effect for this varied literature is not a promise that any comment, grade, or praise improves learning. [Meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087).

**Application:** request feedback on a specific attempt and its reasoning. “Where does this explanation of the experiment stop following from the evidence?” asks for information that can change the next attempt. “Is this good?” leaves the required information unspecified. These prompts illustrate informative feedback; their wording has not been separately tested.

After correction, an unaided attempt checks what is now available to the learner. That combines feedback with [[The testing effect|retrieval practice]]; it does not follow that merely reading feedback produces the same result.

## Asking for help is a learning behaviour

Ryan & Pintrich (1997) studied adolescents' help seeking in maths. Perceived threats and benefits were related to whether learners avoided help; benefits also predicted adaptive help seeking. This is evidence about associations in a particular student population, not a trial showing a particular help-request script improves adult learning. [Paper](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.89.2.329).

**Application, also grounded in [[Cognitive load and worked examples|instructional guidance]]:** present the attempt and identify the point that is unclear: “I can follow the first transformation, but not why this assumption permits the second.” Ask for the explanation or example needed to proceed, then try again. Help that supplies missing understanding can support independent performance; independence is assessed on the subsequent attempt, not by whether help was ever used.

## Peer discussion has evidence; mere company is a different claim

Smith et al. (2009) studied individual answers, peer discussion, and a subsequent individual answer to a similar conceptual question in undergraduate genetics. Performance on the new question supported gains in understanding beyond simply adopting another student's answer. This is a specific instructional procedure with a limited domain and outcome, not proof that any study group helps. [Paper](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1165919).

**Application:** each person first attempts a question, discusses the reasoning, then answers a related question independently. For an adult learning a technical topic, that is an extrapolation of the studied procedure. The individual follow-up distinguishes shared agreement from independent capability.

This paper does not establish benefits from silent coworking or “body doubling.” Those are separate interventions and require their own evidence. They are not prescribed here on the strength of peer-instruction results.
