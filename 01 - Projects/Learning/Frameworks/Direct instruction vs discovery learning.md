# Direct instruction vs discovery learning

One of the most practically important and most muddled questions in the field: should a new domain be learned by exploring and discovering it, or by explicit, guided instruction? The answer is conditional on how much the learner already knows, and the muddle comes from a specific non-sequitur worth naming first.

## The non-sequitur

*Constructivism as epistemology* — the claim that learners actively build knowledge rather than absorbing it whole — is broadly true of how the brain works. *Constructivism as pedagogy* — the claim that one should therefore teach by discovery, with minimal guidance — does not follow from it. "The brain constructs knowledge, so teach by having learners construct it themselves" is the most common misunderstanding in this area. How knowledge is built internally says nothing about whether the learner should be handed the structure or made to find it.

## The core finding

Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006), "Why Minimal Guidance During Instruction Does Not Work," argued from human cognitive architecture: minimal-guidance methods (pure discovery, unguided problem-based and inquiry learning) ignore working-memory limits, so a novice spends capacity searching the problem space instead of building schemas, and little gets encoded. For **novices**, worked examples and explicit guidance reliably beat unguided discovery. This is the same [[Cognitive load and worked examples|cognitive-load]] logic as the worked-example effect, and Project Follow Through (the large US comparison in which Direct Instruction outperformed the alternatives) is the classic field support.

## The conditionality, and the rebuttal

The claim is bounded, not "explicit instruction always wins." Its own mechanism — the [[Cognitive load and worked examples|expertise-reversal effect]] — says the advantage of heavy guidance recedes and then reverses as prior knowledge grows: once the schema is in place, provided guidance becomes redundant load. So the prescription is that guidance level should track expertise.

The substantive rebuttal (Hmelo-Silver, Duncan & Chinn, 2007) is that *well-designed* problem-based and inquiry learning is heavily scaffolded, so Kirschner et al.'s critique really lands on *unguided* discovery, not on scaffolded inquiry, which does work. Both sides converge on the same moderator: prior knowledge. Pure discovery is poor for novices; scaffolded problem-based learning can be good; and as expertise rises, learners need and benefit from less guidance.

## Verdict

Validated for the core, bounded claim: for novices in a domain, guided or explicit instruction beats pure discovery — robust and replicated. The critics are right that the real target is *minimal* guidance (not all inquiry), that well-scaffolded inquiry can work, and that the prescription reverses with expertise. The leap from "constructivism is how we learn" to "therefore teach by discovery" is the part to reject.

## Application

The application to a self-directed adult is to establish relevant prior knowledge before reducing guidance. Professional experience in an adjacent field is not by itself evidence that the novice caveat is weak. Learning go-to-market through a campaign with a template is one possible scaffolded application, not a demonstrated universal best mode. But in a genuinely *new* sub-domain where no schema exists — the mechanics of a specific ad platform, enterprise contract structure, a regulatory regime — explicit instruction comes first: worked examples, templates, an annotated expert walkthrough, then a switch to live practice once one correct instance can be executed. Trying to "discover" the fundamentals of something with no foothold is the slow, overloaded path. This is the practical core of [[Project-based and just-in-time learning]].
