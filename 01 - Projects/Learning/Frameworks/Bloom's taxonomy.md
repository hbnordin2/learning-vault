# Bloom's taxonomy

A classification scheme for **educational objectives and assessment items**. It is among the most-invoked and most-misread frameworks in the field. Bloom et al. (1956) sorted cognitive goals into six levels: Knowledge → Comprehension → Application → Analysis → Synthesis → Evaluation. The Anderson & Krathwohl (2001) revision turned the nouns into verbs, swapped the top two, and made *Create* the apex: Remember → Understand → Apply → Analyse → Evaluate → Create. It is a taxonomy of objectives, not a tested theory of how people learn, and most popular use treats it as the latter.

## What it is for

Its legitimate uses concern designing instruction and assessment, not modelling cognition: giving teachers a shared vocabulary for goals, writing learning objectives, building tests that span a range of cognitive demands, and aligning a curriculum so the assessment matches the stated aims. For those uses it is genuinely useful and near-universal.

The substantive part of the 2001 revision is easy to miss. It split the scheme into a two-dimensional **Taxonomy Table**: a cognitive-process dimension crossed with a *knowledge* dimension (Factual / Conceptual / Procedural / Metacognitive). That split was an admission that the 1956 version conflated what is being learned (content) with what is being done with it (process).

## Is the hierarchy empirically validated? No

The common classroom reading — that the six levels form a cumulative ladder climbed in order, each presupposing the ones below — has weak-to-no empirical support:

- Seddon's (1978) validity review found the evidence does not establish the cumulative hierarchy. Kreitzer & Madaus (1994): "no one has been able to validate the hierarchical structure" (though it has not been decisively disproven either).
- Early tests found the order does not hold: Synthesis and Evaluation effectively swap in difficulty (relevant to the modern doubt about whether "Create" is really harder than "Evaluate"), and a single underlying factor predicts performance across all six levels (Madaus et al., 1973). Non-adjacent levels link directly, violating the ladder.
- Larsen et al. (2022) tested the *revised* taxonomy on 940 biology items: the two dimensions are not independent, and action verbs do not reliably predict cognitive process. The common "use these higher-order verbs to write higher-order objectives" technique does not map cleanly onto what students actually do.

## Verdict and how to use it

A useful design and communication tool; the cumulative-hierarchy-as-learning-sequence reading is unvalidated. Treating "climb Bloom's ladder in order" as a developmental path imports a claim the framework was never validated to make.

For a self-directed adult, the genuinely useful move is diagnostic: ask what level the learner is actually operating at on a topic. Can the learner only *recall* the definition of a go-to-market motion, or also *apply* it to a funnel, *analyse* why a competitor's is different, and *create* a new one? That question exposes the gap between recognition and usable competence, the same gap [[The metacognitive trap|fluency]] hides and that [[Transfer of learning|transfer]] is about. The ladder should not be trusted as a sequence to march up, and verb-based objective-writing should not be trusted as if the verb guaranteed the cognition.
