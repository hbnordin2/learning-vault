# Expertise, schemas, and chunking

Expertise is **reorganised, pattern-indexed knowledge in long-term memory** — not more raw memory, and not faster general processing. Much of the rest of the project rests on this fact. It explains why [[Cognitive load and worked examples|element interactivity]] falls as learning proceeds (a schema collapses many interacting elements into one chunk), why [[Transfer of learning|transfer]] rides on shared deep structure, and why guidance that helps novices hurts experts (the expertise-reversal effect). It is also where the popular phrase "building mental models" gets a precise answer.

## Chunking and the chess studies

The canonical evidence is Chase & Simon (1973), building on de Groot (1965): chess masters reconstruct a *real game* position far better than novices after a five-second glance, but with a *random* arrangement of pieces the master's advantage nearly disappears. Masters do not have better raw memory; they perceive the board in larger meaningful **chunks** (familiar configurations), estimated at tens of thousands. Expertise is reorganised knowledge, not superior general memory.

One correction the textbook version usually drops: Gobet & Simon (1996) showed the skill advantage on random positions is small but statistically significant, not zero, so pure chunk theory cannot fully explain it. They revised it into **template theory**: experts use long-term-memory retrieval structures ("templates") with slots for variable information, not just static chunks. Chase & Simon's direction is robust and replicated; the strong "advantage vanishes entirely on random boards" claim is the part that has been corrected.

## Schemas: organised by deep structure

The complementary finding (Chi et al., 1981): asked to sort physics problems, novices group them by *surface features* (what objects appear, what the diagram looks like) while experts group them by *underlying principle*. Expert knowledge is hierarchical and principled; novice knowledge is flat and surface-bound. This deep-structure organisation is what makes knowledge usable on a new problem — the schema is the shared structure across surface-different cases that [[Transfer of learning|transfer]] depends on.

## Automaticity and the shape of the learning curve

With practice, processing shifts from controlled (slow, effortful, serial) to automatic (fast, effortless, parallel), developing through consistent practice (Shiffrin & Schneider, 1977); Anderson's ACT-R describes the parallel move from declarative knowledge (facts that can be stated, heavy on working memory) to procedural knowledge (skills that are executed) via knowledge compilation. The classic summary that improvement follows a *power law of practice* (Newell & Rosenbloom, 1981) needs one correction: Heathcote, Brown & Mewhort (2000) re-fit thousands of learning curves and found *exponential* functions fit individuals better — the apparent power law is largely an artifact of *averaging* across heterogeneous learners. Improvement decelerates with practice; the specific "power law" is downgraded.

## What "mental models" means

The term carries two unrelated meanings, and conflating them is a category error:

- **The cognitive-science meaning** (Johnson-Laird, 1983): a *mental model* is a working-memory representation whose structure mirrors the situation it represents, built on the fly to reason and draw inferences. In this theory reasoning proceeds by constructing models of the possibilities consistent with what is known; because working memory holds only a few at once, people omit models and make systematic, predictable reasoning errors. It is a specific, falsifiable theory of reasoning with experimental support.
- **The folk or business meaning** (Munger, Farnam Street): a "latticework" of consciously-collected explanatory frameworks (supply and demand, inversion, second-order effects) used as decision heuristics. This is a different thing — deliberately curated conceptual tools, not the unconscious working-memory structures Johnson-Laird studies.

The experimental literature on mental models says almost nothing about whether collecting a Munger-style latticework improves decisions; that claim is essentially untested folk pedagogy borrowing the term's scientific prestige. For *learning a domain*, the relevant sense is neither of these exactly. It is **schema-building**: organising knowledge of the domain by its deep structure and testing it against reality. That is real, it is what these mechanisms build, and it is worth doing. The rigour of Johnson-Laird's research should not be imported to justify a latticework, and collecting frameworks should not be expected to substitute for building accurate schemas of how a domain actually works.

## Verdict

The core phenomenon — expertise as reorganised, schema-indexed knowledge — is robust and unifying. The original chunk mechanism is refined (templates). Automaticity and the declarative-to-procedural transition are solid; the power *law* specifically is downgraded to "decelerating improvement." Johnson-Laird's mental-model theory is a legitimate, productive research programme; the folk "mental models" framing is a motivational scheme, not a validated intervention.
