# Metacognition and self-regulated learning

**Metacognition** is thinking about one's own thinking — monitoring what is known and controlling how it is studied. **Self-regulated learning (SRL)** is the cyclical process of planning, executing, and reflecting on one's own learning. This cluster matters more than its modest intervention effects suggest because of one robust finding: self-monitoring is systematically miscalibrated in a predictable direction, and correcting that miscalibration is the highest-leverage move available to a self-directed learner. The detailed treatment of the miscalibration is [[The metacognitive trap]]; this note covers the broader construct and how much the interventions actually deliver.

## The construct

Flavell (1979) coined "metacognition" and split it into metacognitive knowledge (about the self, the task, and strategies), experiences, goals, and strategies. The dominant formal model is Nelson & Narens (1990): an *object level* (the learning itself) and a *meta level* that **monitors** it (object → meta) and **controls** it (meta → object). Monitoring is where the trouble lies. If the read of how well something is known is wrong, the control decisions (what to restudy, when to stop) are wrong too.

Self-regulated learning (Zimmerman, 2002) frames this as a cycle: forethought (goals, planning) → performance (study, self-monitoring) → self-reflection (self-evaluation, adjustment). It is a process that can be run, not a fixed trait.

## How much do the interventions deliver?

Teaching SRL works, modestly. Dignath & Büttner (2008) found academic gains around d ≈ 0.5–0.6 in school settings, larger when training includes explicit metacognitive reflection; Theobald (2021) found a smaller university-level effect, g ≈ 0.38. Caveats: likely publication-bias inflation, weak self-report instruments (below), and unresolved durability and transfer of the training.

A measurement issue worth flagging: behavioural and event-based measures (what learners actually do, moment to moment) validate metacognition well, but the popular *self-report questionnaires* (the MAI, the MSLQ) correlate poorly with actual studying behaviour (around r ≈ .17–.22). When someone cites "metacognition," it matters whether they mean the behaviour or the questionnaire score.

## The robust core: the feeling of knowing is unreliable

The most replicated strand, and the one that survived the replication crisis intact, is that judgments of learning are inferences from cues rather than direct readouts, and the cues mislead: fluency inflates confidence without improving retention, the most-confident restudy group performs worst at a delay, and learners prefer the methods that work least. The evidence and mechanism are set out in [[The metacognitive trap]]; the practical consequence is that subjective confidence is not a valid signal of competence, so it has to be replaced with objective checks.

## Verdict

The construct is partially validated: the formal and behavioural side is solid, the broad self-report side is weak. SRL interventions are supported with caveats (d ≈ 0.4–0.7, larger with explicit reflection, true effect probably below the point estimates). The calibration failures and illusions of competence are robustly validated, among the most replicated findings in the field.

## The practical upshot

The highest-leverage metacognitive habit is to stop trusting the sense of knowing and route every "is this learned?" judgment through an objective test — closed-book retrieval after a delay (see [[The testing effect]]). Beyond that, the planning-monitoring-reflecting cycle helps modestly, and the most reliable way to calibrate judgment in fuzzy domains is to make predictions and score them (see [[Deliberate practice in fuzzy domains]]). Accurate self-assessment is reached by measurement, not by introspection.
