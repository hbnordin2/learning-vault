# Note-making

Note-making (as distinct from note-taking) is the practice of reformulating ideas in one's own words and storing them in a structured, linkable system designed for long-term use. The two most influential frameworks are the **Zettelkasten** and **evergreen notes**, which share core commitments — atomicity, concept-orientation, dense linking — but differ in origin, vocabulary, and emphasis.

## Core principles

Both frameworks converge on a structural claim: the unit of knowledge management should be the individual concept or claim, not the book, article, or topic. This commitment has three parts:

- **Atomicity.** One idea per note, self-contained enough to be understood without reading other notes. "Atomic" means self-contained and irreducible, not merely short — a note that says "see previous discussion" is short but not atomic; a note that states a claim, defines its terms, and gives an example might be 200 words and fully atomic.
- **Concept-orientation.** Notes are organized by idea, not by source. A note titled "Cue overload degrades recall" accumulates findings from multiple sources; a note titled "Notes on Watkins & Watkins 1975" buries the idea inside a source container.
- **Dense linking.** Notes connect to each other by explicit cross-reference. The linking pressure forces careful thinking about how ideas relate, and creates navigable structure without top-down classification.

## The two traditions

[[The Zettelkasten]] traces to Niklas Luhmann's physical slip-box (~90,000 cards, two collections, organic branching via Folgezettel numbering). [[Evergreen notes]] is Andy Matuschak's reformulation, which shares atomicity and linking but adds explicit developmental trajectory — evergreen notes are "written and organized to evolve, contribute, and accumulate over time, across projects." The distinction between the two is smaller than often claimed; the biggest real difference is emphasis (Luhmann: recombination and surprise; Matuschak: iterative development and concept-oriented titles).

## The evidence question

The cognitive mechanisms these practices invoke — the [[Elaboration and self-explanation|generation effect]] (reformulating in your own words), elaboration (connecting to prior knowledge), and retrieval through linking — are individually well-supported by controlled experiments. But the specific claim that atomicity and dense linking produce better outcomes than other structured formats (concept maps, Cornell notes, well-organized longer documents) has never been tested. The gap between "generation effect exists" and "therefore write atomic evergreen notes" involves several unvalidated inferential steps — see [[The evidence base for structured note-making]].

## Relationship to other systems

**Progressive summarization** (Forte/PARA) works top-down: capture, highlight, bold, summarize. It distills existing material; atomic note-making generates new formulations. The two are complementary — progressive summarization can process sources before extracting atomic notes. **Commonplace books** (Renaissance tradition) collected passages organized by topic; the Zettelkasten's departure is rewriting in one's own words and organizing by associative connection rather than topical classification.
