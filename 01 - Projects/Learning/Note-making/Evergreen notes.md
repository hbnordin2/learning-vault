# Evergreen notes

Evergreen notes are Andy Matuschak's framework for personal knowledge management. The concept overlaps substantially with [[The Zettelkasten]] but is presented as an evolution rather than a replication, with a different emphasis: where Luhmann's system optimizes for recombination and serendipity, Matuschak's optimizes for iterative development of understanding.

## Core properties

- **Atomic.** Each note focuses on a single idea or concept — see [[Note-making]].
- **Concept-oriented.** Notes are factored by concept rather than by source. Organizing by source (e.g., "Notes on Kintsch 1988") means "your new thoughts on the concept don't combine with the old ones to form a stronger whole." Concept-orientation creates productive friction: "when writing new notes, we have to find where they fit into the whole," which surfaces unexpected cross-domain connections.
- **Densely linked.** Links between notes force careful thinking about how ideas relate.
- **Developed over time.** Unlike static reference notes, evergreen notes evolve as understanding deepens. They are "written and organized to evolve, contribute, and accumulate over time, across projects."
- **Titles function as APIs.** Concept-oriented titles (e.g., "Cue overload degrades recall" rather than "Notes on Watkins & Watkins 1975") create strategic entry points. A title that names a claim is more findable and linkable than a title that names a source.

## How it differs from Zettelkasten

Matuschak identifies the shared commitments: "concept-orientation, atomicity, emphasis on linkage." The real differences are:

- **Developmental trajectory.** Evergreen notes are explicitly designed to be revised. Luhmann's cards were added to but rarely rewritten; the system grew by accretion and branching.
- **Digital affordances.** Backlinks, full-text search, and graph views replace Folgezettel numbering and hub notes as the primary navigation mechanisms.
- **Intentional distance from community.** Matuschak notes that most contemporary Zettelkasten practitioners focus on "documenting others' ideas rather than advancing their own creative work," which he sees as a significant divergence from Luhmann's actual practice.

Bob Doto argues the supposed difference between "permanent" (Zettelkasten) and "evergreen" notes is mostly terminological: "permanent" refers to a note's status in the system (having graduated from fleeting notes after refinement), not to immutability. "None of the proposed differences are in any way at odds with the zettelkasten method."

## The spaced repetition connection

Matuschak argues that "maintaining densely linked evergreen notes approximates spaced repetition" because the process requires constantly rereading and revising past writing. The approximation is loose: the process activates the [[Elaboration and self-explanation|generation effect]] (effortful revision) but does not leverage the [[The testing effect|testing effect]] (retrieval without looking at the answer) or the [[The spacing effect|spacing effect]] (systematically scheduled review). Matuschak has separately developed the "mnemonic medium," which embeds spaced repetition prompts directly within prose narrative, as a more deliberate bridge between note-writing and retention.

## Evidence tier

- **The properties and framework**: practitioner theory, not empirical research. Matuschak is a serious researcher (formerly at Apple and Khan Academy), and his arguments are grounded in cognitive science literature, but the evergreen notes framework itself has not been empirically tested.
- **"Note maintenance approximates spaced repetition"**: plausible theoretical argument, untested. The approximation is genuine but crude — it misses the testing and scheduling components that make SRS effective.
- **"Concept-oriented titles are more effective than source-oriented"**: reasonable practitioner claim. No empirical comparison exists.
