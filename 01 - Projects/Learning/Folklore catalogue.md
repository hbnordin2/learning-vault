# Folklore catalogue

Learning is one of the most folklore-ridden domains there is — partly because everyone has done it, partly because the methods that feel effective are mostly the ones that are not (see [[The metacognitive trap]]). This is the explicit catalogue of popular beliefs the research refutes or fails to support, so they can be named and avoided. Each entry flags whether the belief is **REFUTED** (positive evidence against, or a definitive failure to find the predicted effect under proper test) or merely **LOW-UTILITY / UNSUPPORTED** (no good evidence *for* it — which is not the same as disproof). Calibrating that difference is the point; overclaiming a debunking is its own error.

## Learning styles (VAK / VARK matching) — REFUTED

**Claim:** Each person has a preferred sensory modality (visual / auditory / kinaesthetic), and matching instruction to it improves learning.

**Status:** The instructionally meaningful version — the *meshing hypothesis*, that the *best* method depends on the learner's style — is refuted. Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork (2008) isolated it: confirming it requires a crossover interaction in a randomised design (style-A learners do best with method A, style-B with method B), the right studies are almost never run, and the few adequate ones contradict it. Coffield et al. (2004) reviewed 71 style models and found the instruments psychometrically weak. This is the most widespread neuromyth, and it persists overwhelmingly: 89–97% of teachers across many countries endorse it (Dekker et al., 2012; Howard-Jones, 2014), and ~71% of US educators (Macdonald et al., 2017).

**What's true and uninteresting:** people *have* modality preferences. Having a preference does not mean teaching to it helps. The effective variable is matching the *modality to the material* (learn geography from maps, music from audio), not to the learner.

## The learning pyramid / "we remember 10% of what we read…" — REFUTED (fabricated numbers)

**Claim:** A pyramid of fixed retention by activity — 10% of what we read, 20% hear, 50% see-and-hear, 90% of what we do or teach.

**Status:** The numbers are fabricated. Edgar Dale's *Cone of Experience* (1946) was a descriptive ordering of experiences from concrete to abstract and contained *no* percentages; Dale warned against reading it literally. The percentages were grafted on by Treichler (1967), a Mobil Oil trainer who cited no research, and propagated by NTL Institute — which admits in writing it cannot find the original research. Tells: the numbers are suspiciously round and *vary between copies* (top tier sometimes 80%, sometimes 90%). Debunked by Lalley & Miller (2007) and Letrud & Hernes (2016). What's true: retention depends on the cognitive processing a task demands, not a fixed per-channel percentage.

## Left-brain / right-brain learners — REFUTED

**Claim:** People are "left-brained" (logical) or "right-brained" (creative), and this should shape how they learn.

**Status:** Refuted. Nielsen et al. (2013) analysed resting-state scans of 1,011 people: lateralisation is *local* (specific functions lateralise), but there are no globally left- or right-brained individuals. Endorsed by ~86–91% of teachers (Dekker et al., 2012). The related "we only use 10% of our brain" is also false — imaging shows activity across virtually all regions, and damage almost anywhere produces deficits.

## Massed practice / cramming — REFUTED as a retention strategy

**Claim:** Concentrated back-to-back study is an effective way to learn; the fluency felt while cramming means it is working.

**Status:** Refuted for retention (real only for an imminent test). Spaced practice reliably beats massed for durable memory (Cepeda et al., 2006; 317 experiments). Cramming boosts a test taken minutes later, which is exactly why it *feels* productive — but that is retrieval strength, not learning, and it collapses over days. The fluency of cramming is the [[The metacognitive trap|metacognitive illusion]] in a clear form. See [[The spacing effect]].

## Rereading and highlighting — LOW-UTILITY (not refuted)

**Claim:** Rereading the text and highlighting key passages are good ways to study.

**Status:** Low-utility, not disproven. Dunlosky et al. (2013) rate both among the *least* effective of ten common techniques — they create perceptual fluency (the material feels familiar on a second pass) that learners misread as mastery, without the effortful retrieval that builds durable memory. They are the two most-used student strategies and two of the weakest. The accurate framing is "poor use of time relative to retrieval and spacing," not "worthless."

## Multitasking and "digital natives" — REFUTED

**Claim:** Some people, especially young "digital natives," can effectively learn or work on several streams at once.

**Status:** Refuted. Switching between demanding tasks carries measurable costs (Rubinstein et al., 2001); this is not a claim that no activities can ever be performed together. See [[Attention, interruptions, and breaks]] for the experimental scope and practical application. Heavy media-multitaskers are *worse* at filtering distraction and switching, and are overconfident about their ability (Sanbonmatsu et al., 2013) — though the specific Ophir et al. (2009) deficits have had mixed replication, so the task-switch-cost finding is the firmer one. The "digital native" who learns differently and multitasks well does not exist as described (Kirschner & De Bruyckere, 2017; Bennett et al., 2008). The widely quoted "multitasking costs 40% of productive time" figure is a loose popularisation — directionally right, not a robust constant.

## The 10,000-hour rule — REFUTED as popularly stated

**Claim:** ~10,000 hours of practice makes anyone an expert at anything.

**Status:** Refuted as the popular distortion; the underlying construct (deliberate practice) is real but far from sufficient. The figure was an *average* by age ~20 for elite violinists, not a threshold, and Ericsson disowned Gladwell's version. Deliberate practice explains ~1% of professional and ~4% of educational performance variance (Macnamara et al., 2014). The opposite overclaim should also be avoided: practice genuinely matters. Full treatment at [[Deliberate practice]].

## The Mozart effect — REFUTED (popular version)

**Claim:** Listening to Mozart makes a person (or a baby) smarter.

**Status:** The popular version is refuted; the real effect is tiny, transient, and not Mozart-specific. The original (Rauscher et al., 1993) found a ~10–15-minute boost on *one spatial task* in *college students* — not babies, not general IQ, not lasting. The meta-analysis (Pietschnig et al., 2010; >3,000 subjects) finds the effect small and largely driven by publication bias, with no Mozart-specific enhancement; any enjoyable music produces a comparable arousal/mood bump.

## "Study the way that feels easiest" — REFUTED

**Claim:** If a study method feels smooth and easy, it is working; effortful methods are inefficient.

**Status:** Refuted — subjective ease is negatively predictive. The methods that feel hardest (retrieval, spacing, interleaving) work best, and learners systematically prefer the worse, easier-feeling ones: in Kornell & Bjork (2008), spacing/interleaving objectively won but the majority judged massing/blocking more helpful. This is the whole content of [[01 - Projects/Learning/Foundations/Desirable difficulties|Desirable difficulties]] and [[The metacognitive trap]]: fluency is a misleading cue.

## "Testing is just assessment" — REFUTED

**Claim:** A test only measures learning; it does not cause it.

**Status:** Refuted. Retrieval is itself a potent learning event — the [[The testing effect|testing effect]] — and testing produces substantially greater long-term retention than restudying (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006). Every quiz is a learning opportunity, not merely a measurement.

## The pattern

Most learning folklore takes one of three shapes: a *fabricated number* dressed as research (the pyramid percentages, the picture-superiority "d ≈ 2.0", fabricated "Feynman technique" stats); a *real preference or tendency inflated into a law* (modality preferences → learning styles, deliberate practice → 10,000 hours, a tiny arousal effect → the Mozart effect); or a *method that feels effective being mistaken for one that is* (cramming, rereading, highlighting, multitasking). The first is refuted outright; the second needs the popular version attacked while keeping the kernel; the third is the metacognitive trap. When meeting a learning claim, identify which shape it is.

## Quick triage

- **Refuted, attack outright:** learning-styles matching, the learning pyramid's numbers, left/right-brain learners, "10% of brain," "Mozart makes babies smarter," "testing is only assessment," the multitasking digital native.
- **Real-but-distorted, attack the popular version, keep the kernel:** the 10,000-hour rule (deliberate practice is real but insufficient), the Mozart effect (a tiny transient arousal effect exists).
- **Low-utility, don't overclaim:** rereading and highlighting are weak, not useless.
