# Dual coding and multimedia learning

Dual Coding Theory (Paivio) proposes two functionally separate but linked systems — verbal and nonverbal/imagery — so that material coded in both (a labelled diagram, a word plus a picture) leaves two retrieval traces instead of one. Its practical descendant is Richard Mayer's set of multimedia-design principles. This is the one mechanism cluster where the popular effect sizes are badly inflated and several of the famous principles do not replicate, so it warrants more scepticism than the others.

## Dual coding as theory: contested

The empirical backbone is the picture superiority effect — pictures are recognised and recalled better than words (Standing, 1973). The effect is real, but there is no pooled meta-analytic effect size for it; the "d ≈ 1.5–2.0" figure circulating online is uncited folklore. Dual coding is also losing ground as the explanation: a simpler distinctiveness / sensory-semantic account predicts the effect's boundary conditions better (Higdon et al., 2025), the effect persists in people with aphantasia who report no mental imagery (which the two-store theory predicts should abolish it), and Pylyshyn's long-standing propositional critique argues a single code is more parsimonious than two stores. The durable anchors — the concreteness effect, selective dual-task interference between verbal and visual tasks — survive; the two-store architecture is rivalled by simpler accounts. As a study habit ("make mental images of text"), the direct evidence is weak: Dunlosky et al. (2013) rate imagery-for-text and keyword mnemonics *low-utility*.

## Mayer's multimedia principles: real, but smaller than advertised

Mayer's Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning assumes dual channels, limited capacity (this is just [[Cognitive load and worked examples|cognitive load]]), and active processing. The principles are design rules: combine words and graphics, put labels next to what they describe, cut decorative detail, cue what matters. Cromley & Chen's (2025) re-analysis of Mayer's entire own corpus (181 studies) found an overall effect of **g ≈ 0.37**, versus the d ≈ 0.7–1.3 his books advertise — with confirmed publication-bias asymmetry (Egger's z = 6.93), effects that decline year on year (a novelty effect), larger effects when Mayer is first author (allegiance), and a median treatment of 6.8 minutes. Six of his principles are non-significant inside his own data. The best independent synthesis (Noetel et al., 2022; 1,189 studies) puts well-designed multimedia at **g ≈ 0.51**, strongest for procedural and conceptual content.

Which principles hold, by independent meta-analysis:

- **Robust (≈ g 0.4–0.6 independently):** the basic multimedia effect (words + relevant pictures); **coherence** (cutting "seductive details" — interesting but irrelevant extras — helps); **signalling** (cueing the key elements); **spatial contiguity** (put labels on the diagram, not in a separate legend).
- **Fragile or contested:** **modality** ("narrate, don't caption") suffered a double replication failure (Tabbers & van der Spoel, 2011) and reverses when the learner controls the pace; **redundancy** ("never show text and narration together") is roughly null independently and flips by learner — duplicate text actually helps low-prior-knowledge learners and second-language speakers (an expertise-reversal phenomenon, not a property of the materials). Temporal contiguity, segmenting, personalisation, voice, and on-screen pedagogical agents are weaker or near-null.

## What to take from it

The principles are cheap design heuristics worth roughly g 0.2–0.5 in real settings, strongest for novices on complex material under fixed pacing — not the transformative d ≈ 1.0 effects the textbooks claim. They matter most when making something — slides, a diagram, notes, a course for others. They are not a high-leverage study technique: for one's own learning, the generative methods ([[The testing effect|retrieval]], [[Elaboration and self-explanation|self-explanation]]) outrank "look at a good diagram." The redundancy and modality rules should not be applied dogmatically — for genuine novices, more redundancy often helps.

Concretely: when building materials, pair words with a relevant graphic, put labels directly on the diagram, cut decorative visuals, and cue what matters. When studying, do not mistake making pretty visual notes for learning.
