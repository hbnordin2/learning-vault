# The testing effect

Retrieving information from memory — self-testing — produces materially better long-term retention than restudying the same material for the same time. It is the best-evidenced single technique in learning science, and one of only two rated *high-utility* by Dunlosky et al. (2013). It is also called **retrieval practice**. Producing an answer from memory is itself a memory-modifying event, not merely a measurement of what is already stored, which is why the idea that testing is only assessment is a [[Folklore catalogue|misconception]].

## The signature result

Roediger & Karpicke (2006) gave students prose passages, then had them either restudy or take a no-feedback recall test. At a 5-minute delay, restudy produced higher recall (~83% vs ~71%). By one week the pattern had reversed and widened: the tested group recalled ~61%, the restudy group ~40%. This is the characteristic shape of the field — the method that produces higher performance immediately produces lower performance over time, and vice versa. Restudy raises retrieval strength briefly and feels productive; retrieval builds storage strength and lasts (see [[How memory works]], [[Desirable difficulties]]).

## Effect sizes and replication

The evidence here is unusually strong, with multiple large meta-analyses converging:

- **Rowland (2014)** — 159 studies, testing vs. restudy: **g ≈ 0.50**; 81% of comparisons favoured testing. Recall tests outperformed recognition tests. Feedback matters: **g ≈ 0.73 with feedback vs. 0.39 without.**
- **Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017)** — 272 effects: **g ≈ 0.51 vs. restudy**, and **g ≈ 0.93 vs. no-activity filler**; the classroom subset was *larger* (g ≈ 0.67), not smaller.
- Karpicke & Blunt (2011) — retrieval practice outperformed elaborate concept-mapping on a later test, including on inference questions.

The classroom result is notable because most lab effects shrink in the field, whereas the testing effect holds or grows. Ecological validity is excellent.

## Boundary conditions

- **Feedback roughly doubles the effect** when initial retrieval success is low, and rescues failed retrievals. Check the answer afterwards.
- **Difficulty–success tradeoff (inverted-U).** Harder retrieval produces larger gains *if it succeeds*. A test so hard it fails with no feedback teaches little; one so easy it is automatic teaches little. The target is effortful but passable.
- **Recall outperforms recognition.** Free recall and short-answer (where the answer is generated) build more durable memory than multiple-choice recognition, because they demand more effortful retrieval.

## Transfer — real but bounded

The most overstated claim is that testing builds broadly flexible, transferable knowledge. **Pan & Rickard (2018)** (192 transfer effects) found transfer is real — **d ≈ 0.40** — and robust across changed question *formats* and to application/inference items. But the boundary is sharp: transfer to *related-but-untested* material studied alongside the tested content is near-zero. The benefit is strong for what was actually retrieved and for close variants; testing one item should not be assumed to strengthen neighbouring items that were not tested. The things that need to be produced are the things to card and quiz.

## How to use it

- Replace rereading with self-testing wherever possible. After reading, close the source and free-recall the key claims, then reopen and patch the gaps — see [[Learning from a book or course]].
- Put the genuinely atomic, declarative layer into a [[Spaced repetition systems|spaced-repetition system]], which fuses retrieval with spacing.
- Use retrieval as a diagnostic against [[The metacognitive trap|the fluency illusion]]: material that cannot be reconstructed cold is not yet known.
- Have an [[Learning with AI|LLM]] quiz the learner rather than explain to them, so that the retrieving is done by the learner.
