# Elaboration and self-explanation

Two related generative techniques. Instead of taking material in passively, the learner produces something — a reason, a connection, an explanation — that integrates it with prior knowledge. **Elaborative interrogation** is asking "why is this true?" of each fact. **Self-explanation** is explaining, while studying, why a step follows or what something means. Both are rated *moderate-utility* by Dunlosky et al. (2013). Self-explanation is the better-evidenced of the two and the one that targets understanding rather than fact recall.

## Self-explanation

The phenomenon was first observed by Chi et al. (1989): students who learned physics well spontaneously explained the worked examples to themselves — relating each step to the underlying principle — generating roughly three times as many explanatory statements as poor learners (≈52 vs 18) and solving far more problems (82% vs 46%). That study was correlational. Chi et al. (1994) then showed it causally: prompting students to self-explain a biology text improved comprehension, and the gain was largest on questions requiring inference and integration, not simple fact recall. Self-explanation therefore builds the deep, structured understanding that [[Transfer of learning|transfers]].

The meta-analysis is Bisra et al. (2018): 69 effects, ~5,917 participants, overall **g ≈ 0.55** ("potentially powerful"). The main moderator is a [[Desirable difficulties|generative]] one: the benefit is larger when the learner generates the explanation, and is reduced when the correct explanation is also supplied. Being given the answer undercuts the work of producing it. Self-explanation is not additional upside that can be layered on top of everything; it competes with provision.

## Elaborative interrogation

Asking "why would this be true?" and generating a causal answer. The effect is real and replicated (roughly d ≈ 0.42–0.56), with two limits that matter here. First, it is heavily prior-knowledge-dependent: a good "why" can only be generated when the learner already knows enough, so low-knowledge learners benefit little (Woloshyn et al., 1992). Second, most of its evidence comes from factual cued-recall outcomes, not deep comprehension or transfer; Dunlosky et al. flag that the advantage "may not generalise" to transfer tests. As a tool for understanding rather than memorising facts, its support is thinner than self-explanation's.

## The Feynman technique

The popular "Feynman technique" — explain a concept in plain language as if teaching a novice, notice where the explanation stumbles, return and fix the gap — has no direct trial under that name, and the precise effect-size claims circulating online ("34% boost," and similar) are fabricated SEO content. It is a reasonable packaging of two validated mechanisms: self-explanation (g ≈ 0.55 above), and the expecting-to-teach effect — Nestojko et al. (2014) found that merely expecting to teach material (without ever teaching it) produced better-organised recall and better grasp of main points than expecting to be tested. Preparing and actually explaining lands around d ≈ 0.5–0.6. The technique works because its components work; the evidence is in the mechanisms, not the label.

## The principle, and how to use it

The common thread is generation: producing the explanation oneself beats being given it, which is why these belong with [[The testing effect|retrieval]] and [[Desirable difficulties|desirable difficulties]] rather than with passive review.

- While reading, at each significant claim ask: why is this true, how does it connect to existing knowledge, and how would it apply to the situation at hand? (In a founder context: how does this change what to do on Monday?)
- After a worked example, explain the rationale of each step, not just what was done.
- Explain a concept aloud, plainly, as if to someone who does not know it; the points where the explanation goes vague are the real gaps.
- Attempt an explanation before reading the author's; reading it first cancels the generative benefit.
