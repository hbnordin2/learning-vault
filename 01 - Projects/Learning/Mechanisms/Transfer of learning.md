# Transfer of learning

Transfer is applying what was learned in one context to a different one. It is the actual goal of learning — knowledge that can be deployed on a live problem, not just reproduced on the material that was studied — and it is the most overpromised and underdelivered phenomenon in learning science. *Near* transfer (to a very similar context) is reliable; *far* transfer (across domains, surface features, or settings) is specific, fragile, and rare, and its magnitude falls off sharply with the dissimilarity between where the material was learned and where it is applied. A century of evidence converges on this pessimistic conclusion. The constructive side: some transfer can be engineered, at the cost of deliberate design.

## Why far transfer is hard

- **Thorndike & Woodworth (1901)** refuted the "formal discipline" doctrine — the idea that studying hard subjects (Latin, geometry) strengthens general faculties like a muscle. Training to estimate one set of areas barely improved estimating differently-shaped ones; gains were specific to the trained stimuli. Their **theory of identical elements**: training transfers only to the extent that two tasks share components. The muscle metaphor is false.
- **Detterman (1993)**, "transfer as an epiphenomenon," is the field's benchmark skeptical statement: "transfer is rare, and its likelihood is directly related to the similarity between two situations." He notes apparent transfer is often an artifact of the experimenter hinting at the answer, and concludes that to have something learned, that specific thing should be taught.
- **Barnett & Ceci (2002)** provided the taxonomy that disciplines transfer claims — two content dimensions and six context dimensions (knowledge domain, physical, temporal, functional, social context, modality). Most celebrated "far transfer" successes are modest hops along *one* dimension; transfer that jumps several at once almost never appears.

The clearest modern demonstration is the brain-training collapse. **Owen et al. (2010)**, N = 11,430: people improved enormously on the *trained* tasks (d = 0.73–1.63) with *no* transfer to untrained reasoning/memory (d = 0.01–0.22). **Melby-Lervåg et al. (2016)** found working-memory training yields no convincing far transfer once compared against an *active* control. The recurring methodological problem is that apparent far transfer disappears against a properly matched active control.

## What actually promotes transfer

Transfer is bought with difficulty and design, not with general "brain training":

- **Varied practice / contextual interference.** Practising across varied conditions worsens acquisition but improves transfer to novel variants — the same desirable-difficulty logic as [[Interleaving]].
- **Contrasting cases before telling.** Schwartz & Bransford (1998), "A Time for Telling": students who analysed *contrasting cases* before a lecture transferred to novel problems far better than those who read a summary first — and the advantage appeared *only on transfer*, not on fact recall. Analysing contrasts builds the differentiated structure that prepares the learner to absorb the explanation ("preparation for future learning").
- **Comparison drives abstraction.** Gick & Holyoak (1983): given an analogous story, only ~30% of people spontaneously applied it to a structurally identical problem (~75% once *hinted*). Inducing the abstract schema from a *single* example mostly failed; giving *two* analogues and asking how they are similar reliably induced the portable schema. Abstraction via comparison is the active ingredient, not mere exposure.

## Concrete vs. abstract

Two true facts are in tension here. Concrete material is remembered better (the concreteness effect; Paivio's dual coding — see [[Dual coding and multimedia learning]]), so purely abstract instruction feels inert. But Kaminski et al. (2008) found learners taught an abstract structure with *concrete* surface features bound to the surface and failed to transfer, while abstract-symbol learners transferred well — a result widely cited as "abstract beats concrete." This should not be cited uncritically: De Bock et al. (2011) replicated it and found that *better-designed* concrete examples erased the abstract advantage. The reconciliation is **concreteness fading** (Fyfe et al., 2014): start concrete to ground and motivate, then explicitly and gradually fade to the abstract so the deep structure becomes portable. This captures the encoding benefit of concrete and the transfer benefit of abstract.

## What this means in practice

- Knowledge should not be assumed to travel. Reading about a domain rarely produces the ability to act in it; the gap between understanding something and being able to do it on a live problem is the transfer gap, and it is real.
- To build *transferable* understanding, study several concrete cases of the same underlying structure and explicitly extract what they share — comparison, not a single example. Learn the abstraction *alongside* its instances, not instead of them.
- Practise in conditions close to where the skill will be used, and vary them. The closer the practice context to the application context (Barnett & Ceci's dimensions), the more reliably it transfers.
- Far transfer rides on shared *deep structure*, which is what [[Expertise, schemas, and chunking|schemas]] encode — building the schema is what makes transfer possible at all.
