# Learning

The meta-project: how a self-directed adult can make learning happen, understand a topic, retain it, and apply it. The aim is an applied workflow grounded in research papers, with the findings distinguished from their practical applications and limits. Familiar advice does not qualify as evidence merely because it sounds plausible.

## The one-paragraph answer

Learning has an execution problem as well as a memory problem. [[Designing the learning environment|Changing distracting conditions]], [[Starting and sustaining a learning habit|specifying when and how to start]], and [[Planning and monitoring learning|monitoring progress]] address the former. For retention, [[The testing effect|retrieval practice]] and [[The spacing effect|spacing]] are well-supported defaults. Understanding and skill also require suitable [[Cognitive load and worked examples|instruction]], practice, and [[Feedback, help, and learning with others|feedback]]. [[Sleep and consolidation|Sleep]] supports memory. These are distinct research literatures, not one demonstrated mechanism or a universally optimal recipe; each linked note states the relevant evidence and its boundaries.

## Mechanisms

The techniques, each with its meta-analytic evidence, effect size, and boundary conditions.

- **The high-utility core** — [[The testing effect]] (g ≈ 0.50, the best-evidenced technique) · [[The spacing effect]] (the other high-utility lever; the optimal-gap function).
- **Narrower or encoding-side** — [[Interleaving]] (powerful but narrow — category discrimination) · [[Elaboration and self-explanation]] (the best comprehension technique, g ≈ 0.55) · [[Cognitive load and worked examples]] (managing the working-memory bottleneck; the expertise-reversal boundary) · [[Dual coding and multimedia learning]] (over-hyped; real but small) · [[Transfer of learning]] (the goal of learning, and the most pessimistic literature in the field).

## Frameworks

A validity audit of the major named frameworks, separating tested theory from useful-but-unvalidated organising schemes.

- [[Bloom's taxonomy]] — useful for designing objectives; the learning-hierarchy reading is unvalidated.
- [[Deliberate practice]] — robust core, but the "10,000 hours / talent is a myth" strong form is overstated.
- [[Metacognition and self-regulated learning]] — modest interventions; the calibration-failure findings are the robust, high-value part.
- [[Expertise, schemas, and chunking]] — what expertise actually is (reorganised knowledge), and what "mental models" really means.
- [[Direct instruction vs discovery learning]] — for novices, guidance beats discovery; the prescription reverses with expertise.

## The utility ranking

The anchor result (Dunlosky et al. 2013, with Hattie & Donoghue 2016 converging):

- **High utility:** practice testing, distributed (spaced) practice.
- **Moderate:** elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, interleaving.
- **Low:** summarisation, highlighting/underlining, keyword mnemonic, imagery-for-text, rereading.

This ranking concerns the techniques evaluated in that review. It is not a ranking of every determinant of learning, and cannot establish that environment, instruction, motivation, or feedback matter less. The [[The metacognitive trap|feeling of fluency]] helps explain why rereading can remain attractive despite the benefits of retrieval and spacing.

## Posture and empirical floor

The posture throughout: use research papers, distinguish findings from inference, and state where the evidence is thin or contested. Effect sizes depend on the comparison, population, and outcome; numbers from different literatures are not a universal ranking of leverage. See [[What the science can and can't give you]] and [[Learning evidence and open questions]]. For far [[Transfer of learning|transfer]] and skill in low-validity domains such as [[Deliberate practice in fuzzy domains|market selection]], inspect whether the feedback actually predicts the capability being sought.
