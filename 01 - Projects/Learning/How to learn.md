# How to learn

Learning is confusing partly because so many of its visible signs are inconclusive. You can finish a book, recognise the terminology and follow a demonstration, then sit down to do something with it and discover that you cannot get started. The finished book tells you very little about where that gap is.

I would bring the problem back to what you want to become able to do. That might mean speaking another language, understanding a business, writing software or playing a piece of music. Somewhere between your current ability and that result, something needs to change. The useful question is what it is, and what would show you that it had changed. Completing a course and taking comprehensive notes might help, but neither answers the question on its own.

I would start by separating the work from the evidence that the work is working. Reading is work. Explaining the idea later, without the book doing half the thinking for you, gives you evidence. Following a programming tutorial is work. Building something with the same idea when nobody is supplying the next line tells you considerably more.

Once you make that distinction, the practical decisions become much more tractable. Your attempts can reveal what you need to study. The ability you want determines what your practice needs to require. And if you repeatedly fail to get to the work at all, the circumstances in which you are trying to learn become part of the problem to solve.

The research gives us useful answers about each part of this process. We can use them to build a way of working that responds to what is happening, rather than deciding in advance that every learning problem needs the same treatment. The papers and their limits are in the appendix.

## A better model of learning

Imagine that you watch somebody solve a problem. Every step makes sense. You could explain why the answer is correct. Then you close the video, open a slightly different problem and discover that you have absolutely no idea where to start.

That experience tells you something useful. Following a solution and producing one place different demands on you. In the video, somebody else chose the method, decided which information mattered and arranged the steps in the right order. You understood the decisions after they had been made. The new problem requires you to make them.

The same gap appears when you read. A clear explanation provides the vocabulary, the structure and the connections between ideas. While those supports are visible, understanding can feel effortless. Later, when you have to recover the explanation yourself, you discover which parts were available to you and which parts were being supplied by the page. Familiarity is particularly unhelpful here because it can produce confidence without demonstrating that you can remember or use the material.

We therefore need to distinguish what happens during a learning session from what remains available afterwards. A smooth session can be useful, but smoothness is weak evidence. An awkward attempt can also be useful, provided you have enough knowledge to attempt the task and some way to correct what goes wrong. Difficulty by itself tells us very little. You can struggle for an hour because you are doing valuable practice, or because the explanation omitted something you needed ten minutes in.

The model I would use is a series of attempts that expose a gap, followed by work that addresses that gap. You get enough instruction to make a meaningful attempt, inspect what happens, correct something and return later to see whether the change survived. As you improve, you change the problems and reduce the help so that your ability has to work under the circumstances you actually care about.

Different gaps need different responses. If you cannot remember the vocabulary, you need opportunities to retrieve it. If you know the words but cannot follow the reasoning, you need an explanation or a prerequisite concept. If you can explain the method but cannot execute it, you need practice doing the work. If you can solve the familiar exercise but fail whenever its appearance changes, you need to practise recognising when the method applies.

Doing more of the wrong activity is a remarkably plausible way to stay stuck. Another explanation may feel productive when the missing piece is independent practice. More practice may be equally unhelpful when you are repeating a mistake nobody has identified.

This is why I would organise learning around the next useful attempt. It gives the reading, instruction, memory work and feedback a job. You can then choose among them by asking what is currently preventing you from doing the thing.

## Decide what you want to become able to do

Before choosing a course, describe the result. “Learn data analysis” leaves almost everything undecided. “Take an unfamiliar dataset, answer a business question and explain how much confidence the answer deserves” is much more useful. We can imagine somebody doing that. We can also imagine doing it badly, which means we have something to inspect.

For the rest of this document, suppose that is what you want to learn. You have some experience with spreadsheets, have never used SQL and want to investigate why a company's repeat purchases have fallen. SQL is simply a language for asking questions of data stored in tables. It is one tool you may need; operating the tool is only part of answering the business question.

The end result might be a short analysis that another person can follow: what changed, how you calculated it, what the data can support and what remains uncertain. This immediately reveals several things you need to learn. You have to retrieve the right records, calculate something meaningful and reason about the result. A perfectly functioning query can still support a completely idiotic conclusion.

Now decide the conditions under which the ability needs to work. You will probably have access to documentation, so memorising every SQL command is unnecessary. You will still need enough understanding to recognise what to look up and whether the result makes sense. If the task were a conversation in another language, the balance would be different: stopping to look up every word would interfere with the conversation itself. The conditions determine what needs to be readily available from memory and what can sensibly live in a reference.

Make a small attempt before constructing an elaborate curriculum. In our example, try to calculate how many customers bought again. You may discover that you do not know how the tables relate, that you cannot define “bought again” consistently, or that you have counted purchases when you meant to count customers. Those are different problems. Each gives you a more useful next step than a vague impression that you need to become better at data.

If you cannot even understand the task, begin with an introductory explanation and an example. The point of an early attempt is to locate missing knowledge, not to manufacture a humiliating test you have no chance of passing.

You now have enough of a plan to start: something you want to become able to do, conditions under which it must work, a way to inspect the result and a first gap to address. Keep the plan provisional. Until you have tried the work, some of what you think you need will be a guess.

## Make it possible to do the work

All of this depends on the attempts actually happening. A beautifully designed learning process that exists only while you are thinking about becoming a different person is not going to do much for you.

I would inspect the circumstances before concluding that the problem is your character. If every study session begins on the couch, with your phone lighting up beside you and three other things asking for attention, you have created a situation in which studying has to keep winning a competition. You can change the competition.

This is the useful part of thinking about discipline as design. Research on self-control includes choosing and changing situations before you are deep into resisting a temptation. For learning, the practical application is to make the intended work available and reduce the activities that repeatedly displace it. Have the exercise ready. Silence the messages that do not need an immediate response. Close the conversation you are otherwise going to keep checking. Make the decision while you are arranging the session, rather than asking yourself to make it again throughout the session.

Working somewhere other than home can be useful for exactly this reason. If home means chores, games, interruptions or an easy retreat to the sofa, a library or office may change enough of those conditions to make working easier. You do not need to turn this into a theory that houses are bad for concentration. A noisy cafe may solve nothing. Choose the setting for the interference it removes, and judge it by whether you actually do the work there.

The next thing to remove is ambiguity about starting. “I should learn SQL” leaves you to decide when, where and what to do every time the thought occurs. “After lunch on Tuesday, I will sit at the desk and attempt the repeat-purchase query” connects an identifiable occasion to an action. Plans of this kind have research support. The practical advantage is that the plan contains a start you can recognise.

Repeatedly starting in a consistent context can also make starting more automatic. Be precise about what that buys you. The routine can help you sit down and open the work; it will not automatically solve the statistical problem. There is also no fixed number of days after which you become a disciplined person. A missed session is a reason to inspect what happened and resume, rather than declare the previous month invalid.

Once you are working, distinguish moving around inside the task from abandoning it. Switching between a query and the documentation can be necessary. Switching to an argument in a group chat introduces another task. Experiments on task switching show costs, and notifications can disrupt attention even when you do not answer them. You do not need a mythical number of lost minutes to justify removing an interruption that serves no useful purpose.

If an interruption is unavoidable, leave yourself a short note describing where the work stands and what comes next. “The query runs; next check whether the join duplicated customers” is enough. Research on return plans gives us a reason to use this, and the note has an obvious practical function: it preserves the next decision while you are away.

### When you keep avoiding it

Sometimes the surroundings are fine and you still do everything except begin. Inspect the particular task you are avoiding. Perhaps you cannot tell what the next action is. Perhaps you are missing the knowledge needed to take it. Perhaps the activity matters very little to you. Or perhaps beginning means encountering uncertainty you can temporarily escape by doing something else.

That last possibility matters. Research on procrastination describes how avoiding an unpleasant task can serve immediate mood repair. This does not explain every delay, but it makes a useful question available: what becomes uncomfortable when you actually start?

Answering that question may reveal a practical change. An unclear assignment needs a clearer next action. A problem beyond your current knowledge needs instruction. If the discomfort is making an imperfect attempt that someone else will inspect, more preparation may simply postpone the same encounter. Choose an attempt small enough to examine and correct. You are trying to obtain information, so it is allowed to reveal something you cannot yet do.

You also do not need to enjoy every repetition. Being interested in an activity and personally valuing what it enables are different forms of motivation. A dull exercise can still serve an outcome you care about. That gives it a reason to exist; it does not mean a sufficiently inspiring goal will cure every difficulty getting started.

Finally, allow for fatigue. Short breaks have clearer evidence for reducing fatigue than for making every kind of demanding work better, so choose a workable rhythm instead of treating a timer as a law of cognition. Protect sleep around learning too. Sleep supports memory, and severe sleep loss can impair learning new material. More hours awake are not automatically more hours of useful progress.

## Learn enough to make a useful attempt

Once the session exists, the next question is how much help you need. I would decide that from your knowledge of the particular task. Being an experienced professional does not make you an experienced statistician. You can be very capable and still need somebody to explain what a term means.

When the material is unfamiliar, a good worked example can save you from trying to invent a procedure while simultaneously discovering its parts. Study how somebody solves a relevant problem, including why they make each decision. Then attempt a related problem yourself. As the procedure becomes usable, reduce the support. Instruction that helps a beginner can become redundant when the learner already understands what it supplies.

For our analysis, you might study an example of joining a customer table to an orders table. A join connects records from different tables using a shared value, such as a customer ID. The interesting part is what that does to the data. A customer with five orders can appear five times in the joined result. If you then count rows and call the answer “number of customers,” your query may run perfectly and still be wrong.

That is the kind of explanation you want. It exposes a decision and its consequence. Merely copying the query would let you reproduce the output without understanding what had been counted.

Pause and explain the operation yourself. Why are these tables being connected? What does one row now represent? What would change if a customer had no orders, or several? Then check your explanation against the example and the actual data. Self-explanation has research support, but the checking matters: an elegant explanation of the wrong thing is still wrong.

If you cannot explain a step, get help with that step. You may need the meaning of a term, a simpler example or an explanation of something the author assumed you knew. This is more precise than either rereading the whole chapter indefinitely or deciding the entire subject is beyond you.

Comparison helps you work out which parts of an example matter. Look at two joins that use the same underlying idea but produce different numbers of rows. Identify what changed and why. Then inspect a case where that operation would be inappropriate. You are trying to recognise the conditions under which a method makes sense, so vary those conditions deliberately. Research on analogical learning supports comparing cases and extracting their shared structure; the particular examples still need to be correct.

A course is useful when it supplies this progression: prerequisites, explanations, worked examples and opportunities to attempt the work. Choose it partly by whether its exercises resemble the ability you want. If you only need one part now, you can use that part. If every page assumes knowledge you lack, the apparently slower introductory resource may give you a much more direct route through the problem.

There is no virtue in discovering everything yourself. There is also no evidence of independent ability in having somebody else make every decision. Adjust the help so that you can attempt something meaningful, then find out what remains possible when the help is reduced.

## Make the learning survive the session

Suppose you now understand the join and can complete a related exercise. That is progress. We still need to know whether you can recover the reasoning next week, when the example is no longer fresh and another problem looks slightly different.

This is where retrieval practice earns its place. Put the source away and try to produce something from memory: an explanation, an answer, a diagram or the reasoning behind a procedure. Afterwards, compare it with the source and correct what is missing or wrong. Across many studies, retrieving studied material improves later retention compared with studying it again. The attempt both reveals what is available and provides another opportunity to learn it.

Notice what this changes about reading. You can stop after a useful section and ask yourself what it explained, why the explanation works and when you would use it. You then have something to compare with the page. Rereading alone does not force that comparison, because the missing pieces are supplied as you encounter them.

The same applies to notes. Write an explanation from memory, inspect the gaps and repair it. If a diagram helps you express the relationships, use one. You can still keep reference notes and look things up. The important distinction is whether you are building a resource to consult or checking what you can recover without it. Both can be useful; they answer different questions.

Be sensible about failure. If you have barely encountered an idea, staring at a blank page may reveal very little beyond that fact. Return to the explanation, get enough understanding to attempt the question and try again. If you recover a plausible but incorrect answer, correct it. The goal is repeated opportunities to retrieve and improve, not repeated opportunities to rehearse confusion.

Now separate some of those opportunities in time. Spaced practice generally produces better durable retention than concentrating the same practice into one block. This matters because a session can end with something readily available that will be much less available later. Returning creates another occasion to recover it and inspect what survived.

You do not need to begin with an elaborate scheduling system. Put a later attempt in your calendar, try it and use the result to decide what needs more work. The useful gap depends on the material, how well it has been learned and how long you need to retain it. A review tomorrow and another several days later can be a starting arrangement, but those dates are a choice to test, not a universal optimum discovered by science.

Flashcards are convenient when there is particular information you need to recall. Vocabulary, distinctions between concepts and the conditions under which a rule applies can all give you meaningful questions. Write prompts whose answers you can assess. If a card keeps failing, inspect whether you understand the idea and whether the prompt is asking one intelligible question. Adding more cards will not resolve an ambiguous one.

A deck still covers only what its questions require. Remembering a definition of customer retention does not establish that you can calculate it correctly or recognise when two businesses are using incompatible definitions. For connected reasoning, retrieve the explanation. For performance, attempt the performance. Use cards where they serve the work rather than turning the subject into cards because the software makes that easy.

## Turn practice into better performance

Practice becomes more useful when you can tell what a particular attempt revealed. “I spent two hours analysing data” tells you about the time spent. “My result double-counted customers because I misunderstood the join” tells you what needs to change.

Get feedback at that level. Sometimes you can compare with a correct answer, inspect the records or run a test. Sometimes you need a teacher, coach or colleague who can recognise something you cannot yet see. Ask them to look at the attempt and explain the important error. “This analysis is weak” leaves you guessing. “The two groups had different amounts of time to place a second order” identifies a reason your comparison may be misleading.

Feedback varies enormously in usefulness. A confident opinion is not automatically an accurate diagnosis, and a correction has limited practical value if you cannot understand what to change. Look for information about the work, the relevant standard and the reasoning behind the correction. Then make another attempt in which that correction has to be used.

In our example, suppose newer customers appear to make fewer repeat purchases. Someone points out that they have only had a week to return, while older customers have had a year. The immediate next step is to define a comparable period, such as whether each customer bought again within thirty days of their first purchase, and make sure each included customer has had that opportunity. This is an illustrative business definition, not a universal definition of retention. The useful lesson is that the comparison has to mean what you claim it means.

You might need to isolate one part before returning to the whole analysis. Practise identifying duplicated rows on a tiny dataset you can inspect by hand. Once you understand that operation, use it inside the larger task again. The small exercise lets you examine a particular error; the complete task requires you to notice when that error matters. Success in one does not establish success in the other.

As methods become familiar, change the decisions the exercises require. A page of identical problems tells you which method to use before you begin. A mixture can require you to choose. This is one reason interleaving can help: related problem types are mixed so that you practise distinguishing them. The benefits depend on the material, so use it where selecting among methods is part of the skill. Constantly switching to unrelated subjects is a different activity.

Vary the appearance of the problem too. Use a different dataset, ask a changed question or include a case where the familiar method gives a misleading result. Explain which principle still applies and which assumptions have changed. This gives you evidence about transfer: whether something learned in one setting can be used in another. You should test that transfer rather than assume it from success on the original exercise.

Other people can help without taking over. Try a question independently, discuss the reasoning and then answer a related question alone. Research on structured peer discussion supports arrangements like this. The independent answer matters because a group can arrive at a correct result while leaving you unsure how it got there. Use the discussion to make your next attempt more capable.

### When the feedback is difficult to interpret

Some subjects make this process much harder. You can inspect whether a query counts customers correctly. You cannot inspect a business decision in quite the same way. The outcome may arrive months later, and changes in the market may have helped or hurt you independently of the quality of the decision.

Research on intuitive expertise emphasises two things: an environment with patterns that can actually be learned, and sufficient opportunity to learn them from feedback. Repetition and confidence alone cannot establish that those conditions exist. A person can have extensive experience in a noisy environment without having a reliable ability to predict its next outcome.

In those settings, I would make the reasoning inspectable before the result is known. Write down what you expect, why you expect it and what would make you change your mind. Compare cases, seek criticism from someone who understands the domain and distinguish an explanation you had beforehand from one invented after the outcome. Those records are a practical way to examine judgment; they do not remove uncertainty or turn every domain into an exercise with an answer key.

This is also a reason to be precise about what you have become good at. Being excellent at one part of a field gives you something to build on. It does not settle every adjacent question. When the task changes, inspect the new demands.

## Put the whole thing to work

We can now return to your attempt to understand repeat purchases and work out what a sensible sequence looks like.

In the first session, you try to answer the question and discover that you cannot connect customers with their orders. You study an explained join, use a tiny dataset to see what it does and attempt a related exercise. Then you explain why the result has more rows than the customer table and check that explanation against the data.

That produces a specific next step. At the end of the session, a useful note might read: “I can connect customers and orders, but I need to check whether I am counting people or purchases. Next time, explain the duplication problem from memory, then calculate repeat customers on the small dataset.” It is short because its job is to change the next session, not commemorate this one.

At a later session, you try that without the example open. If the reasoning has disappeared, revisit it and arrange another attempt. If it survives, use it in the larger analysis. The result now exposes a different problem: customers have had unequal opportunities to buy again. You learn to define a comparable period, revise the analysis and ask someone to inspect the reasoning.

Eventually you try a different dataset and a different business question. Perhaps the calculations work, but you cannot explain what the result does and does not show. The next learning need is interpretation. More SQL drills would address something you can already do while leaving the important problem untouched.

That is how the plan should develop. Each attempt supplies information about what to study or practise next. Returning across time tests what remains available. Changing the task tests where it can be used. The schedule matters because it creates those occasions; completing the schedule is not the final measure of success.

Keep enough of a record to make those decisions. What were you trying to do? Where did the attempt fail? What did the feedback reveal? What will you change? Research supports monitoring progress, but you do not need to build an administrative department around it. A few useful sentences can be enough.

You also need a stopping rule. For a bounded task, decide what evidence would make you comfortable moving on: meeting the relevant standard on unfamiliar examples, after a delay, with the assistance you will actually have. Some capabilities will need occasional maintenance. An evolving professional field will require new learning as the work changes. The standard comes from what you need the ability for, not from an obligation to finish every available resource.

The outcome we want is that an unfamiliar subject becomes easier to approach. You can identify an initial task, find out what it assumes, get enough help to try it and use the result to choose your next move. You will still have to learn the subject. You will have a more useful way to begin, and something better than the feeling of being busy to tell you whether you are getting anywhere.

## Questions that tend to come up

### What if I understand it until I try it myself

Then you have found a gap worth working on. Try to locate the first decision you cannot make without the example. Get an explanation of that decision, attempt a related problem and gradually reduce the help. Watching the entire explanation again may be useful, but inspect the particular point where your own reasoning stops.

### What if I keep forgetting everything

Check whether you are actually trying to retrieve anything between exposures. Seeing the answer repeatedly can make it familiar without demonstrating recall. Include attempts from memory, check them and return on separate occasions. If retrieval keeps failing completely, revisit whether the explanation made sense and whether the question is asking for more than you have learned. Difficulty is information about the next step, not proof that the current approach is effective.

### Can I use AI for all of this

You can use it to explain a prerequisite, generate an example, question your reasoning or suggest an exercise. Check important claims and worked answers against reliable material. Then inspect what you can do after the assistance is removed. Studies of AI tutoring have found different outcomes under different designs, including benefits from a structured tutor and harms to later unaided performance with less constrained assistance. A fluent conversation does not settle which experience you are having.

If the point of an exercise is to learn how to make a decision, having the tool make it removes an opportunity to practise that decision. If the point is to produce a result using tools, practise using them and evaluating their output. Decide which ability you are trying to build before asking the tool to do the work.

### Do I have to finish the course before starting a project

No. You need enough prerequisite knowledge to make a useful attempt, and you need to return for instruction when the work exposes something you do not understand. An early project can show you why the instruction matters. A course can reveal dependencies you would otherwise miss. Use each where it helps, and avoid turning either completion or unguided struggle into a requirement for its own sake.

### How do I know whether I need persistence or a different approach

Look at what successive attempts are telling you. Are particular errors being corrected? Can you explain more of the reasoning? Does anything survive a delay? If so, you have some evidence that the work is helping, even when it feels awkward. If the same failure keeps recurring, change something relevant: the explanation, the size of the task, the feedback or the conditions under which you are trying to work. Simply demanding more effort does not identify the missing piece.

You are allowed to be bad at something while you learn it. The useful commitment is to keep making your attempts more informative and giving yourself a chance to correct them. Start with a task small enough to inspect. Find out what you need. Then come back and see what changed.

## Research appendix

### Scope and interpretation

The argument combines findings into a practical approach; the complete approach has not been tested as one intervention. The data-analysis example, suggested records, review dates and stopping decisions are applications of the research, not independently validated prescriptions. Studies differ in participants, tasks, controls and follow-up periods. Classroom and laboratory results therefore do not establish the size of a benefit in adult professional work or every physical skill. The sources are grouped by the claims they support, including the practical questions at the end.

### Defining the task and monitoring progress

[Paper](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000025). Supports progress monitoring across varied goals, not a particular learning-brief or journal format.

Bjork, R. A., Dunlosky, J., & Kornell, N. (2013). Self-regulated learning: Beliefs, techniques, and illusions. *Annual Review of Psychology, 64*, 417–444. [Paper](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-113011-143823). Reviews the distinction between immediate performance and learning, and misleading judgments of knowing.

Rowland, C. A. (2014). The effect of testing versus restudy on retention: A meta-analytic review of the testing effect. *Psychological Bulletin, 140*, 1432–1463. [Paper](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559). Supports retrieval relative to restudy; feedback and the retrieval task matter.

Cepeda, N. J., et al. (2006). Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks: A review and quantitative synthesis. *Psychological Bulletin, 132*, 354–380. [Paper](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354). Supports separated practice, with results depending on the intervals and retention target.

Brunmair, M., & Richter, T. (2019). Similarity matters: A meta-analysis of interleaved learning and its moderators. *Psychological Bulletin, 145*, 1029–1052. [Paper](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000209). Interleaving benefits depend on materials and discrimination demands.

Gick, M. L., & Holyoak, K. J. (1983). Schema induction and analogical transfer. *Cognitive Psychology, 15*, 1–38. [Paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285%2883%2990002-6). Experiments support comparison and abstraction in analogical problem solving.

Barnett, S. M., & Ceci, S. J. (2002). When and where do we apply what we learn? A taxonomy for far transfer. *Psychological Bulletin, 128*, 612–637. [Paper](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.128.4.612). Distinguishes dimensions of transfer; success in one context does not establish success across them.

### Correction, assistance, and judgment

Wisniewski, B., Zierer, K., & Hattie, J. (2020). The power of feedback revisited: A meta-analysis of educational feedback research. *Frontiers in Psychology, 10*, 3087. [Paper](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087). Substantial variation means feedback cannot be treated as one consistently beneficial treatment.

Smith, M. K., et al. (2009). Why peer discussion improves student performance on in-class concept questions. *Science, 323*, 122–124. [Paper](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1165919). Supports a specific peer-instruction procedure, not every form of group study.

Kahneman, D., & Klein, G. (2009). Conditions for intuitive expertise: A failure to disagree. *American Psychologist, 64*, 515–526. [Paper](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016755). Reviews conditions for skilled intuition. The judgment records suggested in the main text are applications, not a separately validated universal procedure.

Kestin, G., et al. (2025). AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning: An RCT introducing a novel research-based design in an authentic educational setting. *Scientific Reports, 15*, 17458. [Paper](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6).

Bastani, H., et al. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *PNAS, 122*, e2422633122. [Paper](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122). Together, these AI studies show the importance of instructional design and independent assessment; they do not isolate one universal prompt or establish long-term professional benefits.
